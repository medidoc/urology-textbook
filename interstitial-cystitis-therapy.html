Treatment of the Bladder Pain Syndrome and Interstitial Cystitis

  • Interstitial cystitis: definition, causes, symptoms and diagnosis
  • Interstitial cystitis: medical treatment and surgical therapy

Conservative Nonpharmacological Therapy

The following interventions are used alone or alongside drug therapy to alleviate the symptoms of bladder pain syndrome. The evidence is uncertain.

Pelvic floor relaxation exercises:

Biofeedback, stress reduction, and warm baths may alleviate symptoms.

Behavioral therapy:

Bladder training can increase functional bladder capacity and thereby reduce urinary frequency. It is suitable for patients with frequency when pain is not the predominant symptom.

Acupuncture:

May improve individual symptoms, although objective parameters have not been consistently affected.

Dietary measures:

Patients are advised to identify and avoid individual symptom-triggering foods and drinks, particularly caffeine, alcohol, sweeteners, hot spices, and beverages that acidify the urine.

Drug Therapy

Except for pentosan polysulfate, none of the drugs listed below is approved for the treatment of interstitial cystitis; their use is off-label.

Pentosan polysulfate (PPS):

Pentosan polysulfate (PPS) is a semisynthetic heparinoid derived from plant bark. The dosage is 100 mg 1-1-1 p.o. PPS is excreted by the kidneys, binds to the urothelial GAG layer, and is thought to reduce urothelial permeability. A treatment effect may not be apparent until after 3–6 months of therapy. Randomized trials and meta-analyses show a moderate benefit. Because of relevant adverse effects, particularly dose- and duration-dependent pigmentary maculopathy, some professional societies advise against PPS therapy (Doiron et al., 2025).

Vaginal estrogen therapy:

In postmenopausal women, vaginal estrogen therapy alleviates bladder irritation symptoms and reduces urinary tract infections; it should be offered as baseline treatment. Vaginal preparations, such as capsules, creams, or estrogen-releasing rings, are more effective and have fewer adverse effects than oral hormone replacement therapy. Dosage: 0.03–0.5 mg of intravaginal estriol once weekly.

Hydroxyzine:

An H1 antihistamine with sedative effects. The dosage is 25 mg 0-0-1 p.o. for one week, followed by an increase to 1-0-2 for three months. It may be considered particularly in patients with signs of allergies; however, a randomized trial did not demonstrate efficacy (Sant et al., 2003).

Amitriptyline:

A tricyclic antidepressant also used to treat neuropathic pain. The dose is gradually increased from 25 to 75 mg 0-0-1. Its effects are mediated by anticholinergic, antihistaminic, and adrenergic mechanisms. One-third of patients report sustained symptom improvement. Sedation is the most common adverse effect. Caution: cardiac toxicity is possible.

Pain therapy:

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may be used, taking their long-term adverse effects into account. For refractory pain, multimodal pain therapy according to the WHO analgesic ladder is indicated.

Immunosuppressants:

Cyclosporine may be effective in refractory cases. Because of its relevant adverse effects, its use should be limited to specialized centers and carefully selected patients. A concurrent autoimmune disease may support its use.

Antibiotics:

Antibiotics should be used only when the urine culture is positive or as a single empirical course at the initial presentation of symptoms. Their efficacy is low in the absence of a documented urinary tract infection.

Unproven drug therapy:

Positive results have been reported in numerous small, uncontrolled studies of drugs that have not been validated by other research groups in prospective or randomized studies. These include L-arginine (nitric oxide donor), anticholinergics (for concomitant overactive bladder), misoprostol (oral prostaglandin), montelukast (leukotriene antagonist), prednisolone, nifedipine (calcium channel blocker), and cimetidine (H2 antihistamine).

Intravesical Therapy

Bladder hydrodistention:

Hydrodistention under anesthesia is primarily of diagnostic value for measuring bladder capacity and determining the disease phenotype. Some patients may experience symptom improvement. The mechanism of action and durability of benefit are unclear.

Intravesical instillations to regenerate the GAG layer:

The aim is to improve the urothelial barrier. Pentosan polysulfate, heparin, chondroitin sulfate, or hyaluronic acid may be instilled individually or in combination. Instillations are usually started at weekly intervals and are generally well tolerated. Depending on the response, the intervals may be extended for maintenance therapy.

Treatment is very costly, depending on the product, and is generally not covered by statutory health insurance. Few randomized trials are available; they have shown only limited or statistically nonsignificant effects (Nickel et al., 2012; Madersbacher et al., 2012). In Germany, ready-to-use preparations are approved as medical devices, which has limited the conduct of high-quality controlled trials.

Unproven intravesical therapy:

DMSO, lidocaine and dexamethasone (optionally with iontophoresis and, if appropriate, followed by bladder distention without anesthesia), and injections of botulinum toxin.

Surgical Treatment Options

Endourological therapy:

Transurethral resection or coagulation of Hunner lesions may lead to substantial symptom improvement; recurrences are common and may require repeat procedures. Coagulation of glomerulations or petechiae in the absence of Hunner lesions is not recommended. Laser treatment may also be used for Hunner lesions.

Sacral nerve stimulation:

May improve subjective and objective symptoms, although the evidence is limited to small studies with short follow-up. The same applies to transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS).

Open surgical treatment:

This is a last resort after unsuccessful drug and interventional treatment. Surgery is an option for patients with severe urinary frequency due to markedly reduced bladder capacity (< 250 ml measured under anesthesia). Surgery is less reliable for relieving chronic pain. Chronic pain persists in a significant proportion of patients even after complete cystectomy and urinary diversion.

Supratrigonal cystectomy:

The trigone is preserved, and the bladder is reconstructed using bowel.

Subtrigonal cystectomy:

An alternative to bladder augmentation. The entire bladder is removed as a potential source of symptoms; the disadvantage is the need for a ureterointestinal anastomosis.
