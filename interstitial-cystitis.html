Bladder Pain Syndrome and Interstitial Cystitis: Definition and Etiology

  • Interstitial cystitis: definition, causes, symptoms and diagnosis
  • Interstitial cystitis: medical treatment and surgical therapy

Definitions

Bladder pain syndrome (BPS), also called interstitial cystitis (IC), is a chronic disease of unknown etiology characterized by urinary frequency, nocturia, bladder pain, and reduced bladder capacity (Loch and Stein, 2004).

Definition of the International Continence Society (ICS):

Bladder pain syndrome is suprapubic pain related to bladder filling, accompanied by symptoms such as urinary frequency and nocturia, when the symptoms cannot be explained by a urinary tract infection or other obvious disease. The diagnosis of interstitial cystitis is reserved for patients with typical cystoscopic and histological findings (Abrams et al., 2002).

Definition of the Society for Urodynamics and Female Urology:

Bladder pain syndrome or interstitial cystitis is an unpleasant sensation (pain, pressure, or discomfort) perceived to be related to the urinary bladder, accompanied by lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) lasting more than six weeks, with no evidence of a urinary tract infection or other identifiable cause (Hanno and Dmochowski, 2009).

Historical classification of interstitial cystitis:

The historical classification distinguishes classic interstitial cystitis, with histological signs of inflammation and ulcers visible on cystoscopy, from nonulcerative interstitial cystitis, without classic signs of inflammation or cystoscopically visible lesions. This distinction is relevant when considering invasive transurethral treatment options.

Epidemiology

  • Prevalence is 10–500 per 100 000, depending on the inclusion criteria. Modern studies report higher prevalence rates.
  • Mean age at diagnosis is 40–50 years; the female-to-male ratio is approximately 9:1.

Etiology and Pathophysiology

The etiology of interstitial cystitis is multifactorial and remains unclear. Proposed mechanisms include urothelial barrier dysfunction, neurogenic inflammation, mast cell activation, central sensitization, immunological mechanisms, and fibrotic remodeling with consequent reduction in bladder capacity.

Increased urothelial permeability:

Impairment of the urothelial glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer has been reported in interstitial cystitis and in associated diseases such as ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease. When urothelial permeability is impaired, bacteria and “toxic” or allergenic urinary substances may trigger an inflammatory cascade in the bladder wall. It is unclear whether increased urothelial permeability is a primary step in the pathophysiology of interstitial cystitis or a consequence of the inflammatory cascade.

Infection of the bladder wall:

Attempts to demonstrate a persistent infectious cause of interstitial cystitis have not produced reproducible results. Antibiotic treatment is generally ineffective without evidence of an infectious pathogen. It has been proposed that transient infections may induce a persistent autoimmune response against bladder wall components through molecular mimicry. Increased mast cell infiltration and cytokine concentrations in the urothelium support this hypothesis. Urinary tract infections may act as triggers.

Antiproliferative activity of urine:

Studies have demonstrated antiproliferative activity in the urine of patients with interstitial cystitis. The responsible antiproliferative factor (APF) is thought to be secreted by the urothelium and to belong to the Frizzled protein family. In predisposed patients who are APF-positive, a urothelial lesion due to infection, trauma, or overdistention may initiate bladder pain syndrome. The clinical validity of APF has not been established (Keay, 2008).

Neurogenic inflammation:

Chronic stimulation of afferent C fibers may induce neurogenic inflammation. Increased concentrations of mediators such as substance P, neurokinin A, and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) have been demonstrated in interstitial cystitis. The resulting inflammatory cascade cannot be distinguished phenotypically from immunological reactions. The nociceptive threshold for bladder filling is reduced in patients with IC. Chronic pain stimuli may produce persistent pain signaling through mechanisms of central sensitization at the spinal cord level.

Autoimmunity and IC:

The role of autoimmune processes is unclear. Autoantibodies against bladder antigens have been detected, but their specificity is disputed. A secondary autoimmune phenomenon following tissue destruction is also possible. The therapeutic efficacy of immunomodulatory agents supports the relevance of immunological factors, but the precise role of autoimmunity remains uncertain.

Psychological factors:

In current neuropsychological models, psychosomatic and psychopathological comorbidities are interpreted as manifestations of central sensitization and as responses to the substantial burden of chronic pain (chronic overlapping pain syndrome).

Pathology

Histological findings are nonspecific and variable; histology does not establish the primary diagnosis. A key role of pathology is to exclude malignancy, particularly urothelial carcinoma in situ (CIS).

Macroscopy:

Bladder ulcers (Hunner lesions) and glomerulations (punctate mucosal hemorrhages) may be seen after standardized hydrodistention. Glomerulations have low specificity because they also occur in asymptomatic individuals. In advanced disease, transmural fibrosis may result in irreversible bladder contraction.

Microscopy:

Findings may include mast cell infiltration of the bladder wall (> 28 mast cells/mm2), lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, subepithelial hemorrhages, and urothelial denudation. There is no reliable correlation between histological severity, cystoscopic phenotype, and clinical prognosis.

Signs and Symptoms

Basic symptoms:

Typical symptoms include suprapubic, pelvic floor, or perineal pain that usually increases as the bladder fills and temporarily subsides after voiding. Accompanying LUTS include marked urinary frequency, urgency, and nocturia lasting at least six weeks, without evidence of a causative pathogen.

Symptom scores:

The Interstitial Cystitis Symptom Index (ICSI), Interstitial Cystitis Problem Index (ICPI), and University of Wisconsin IC Scale (UW-IC Scale) are established tools for standardized follow-up and treatment monitoring.

Diagnosis

Bladder pain syndrome is diagnosed from the characteristic symptoms after systematic exclusion of differential diagnoses.

Voiding diary:

A voiding diary documents voided volumes and urinary frequency. Typical findings include voided volumes below 250 ml, intervals between voids shorter than 2 h, and persistent nocturia.

Urine testing:

Urine sediment and culture are used to assess for infection; no causative pathogen should be detected. Urine cytology is used to exclude bladder cancer, particularly carcinoma in situ.

Ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder:

Ultrasound is used to exclude postvoid residual urine, urolithiasis, and intraluminal masses.

Cystoscopy:

Cystoscopy is ideally performed under general or regional anesthesia to inspect the bladder, perform diagnostic hydrodistention (irrigation height 80–100 cm H2O for 2–5 min), measure maximum anatomic bladder capacity, and obtain biopsies when indicated.

Glomerulations:

Punctate, petechial mucosal hemorrhages that appear after the distention medium is drained. They are common but are not pathognomonic for the disease.

Hunner lesions:

Erythematous, scarred areas with radially oriented vessels that typically split and bleed during hydrodistention. They define a clinically relevant lesion phenotype and are important for treatment.

Bladder biopsy:

Suspicious areas should be biopsied after hydrodistention to rule out carcinoma in situ. In patients at high risk for urothelial carcinoma, additional quadrant biopsies may be considered.

Optional investigations:

Urodynamics: In established disease, urodynamics has limited diagnostic value because of pain. A small bladder capacity due to pain may be found. Urodynamics is indicated to exclude overactive bladder.

Potassium chloride test:

Instillation of potassium chloride (0.4 mol/l) into the bladder may cause pain when urothelial permeability is increased. Clinical studies have not demonstrated a benefit of the potassium chloride test for managing patients with bladder pain syndrome.

Differential Diagnosis

  • Infections: bacterial cystitis, schistosomiasis, and tuberculosis.
  • Neurological diseases with urgency symptoms.
  • Chronic prostatitis, prostatodynia, and prostate cancer.
  • Bladder stones.
  • Bladder carcinoma in situ.
  • Gynecological diseases: vaginal estrogen deficiency; cervical, ovarian, or endometrial cancer; infections such as vaginitis or salpingitis; and endometriosis.
  • Radiation cystitis.
  • Adverse effects of medications such as cyclophosphamide.
