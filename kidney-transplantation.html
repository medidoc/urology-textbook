Kidney Transplantation: Indications, Technique, and Prognosis

Kidney transplantation is a renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage kidney disease. Compared with long-term dialysis, it significantly improves survival and quality of life. It requires a suitable recipient, an appropriate donor kidney, and lifelong immunosuppressive therapy. See the EAU guidelines.

Schematic drawing of right-sided kidney transplantation with vascular anastomosis to the iliac vessels.

Epidemiology

Prevalence of kidney transplantation:

In Germany, 2,075 kidneys were transplanted in 2024: 1,443 after deceased donation and 632 after living donation. The prevalence of patients with a functioning kidney transplant in Germany is approximately 45 per 100,000 inhabitants.

Etiology of End-Stage Kidney Disease

See section chronic kidney disease.

Contraindications to Kidney Transplantation

Infections:

Acute, uncontrolled bacterial or viral infections are contraindications until they have been treated. Infections that cannot be eradicated are absolute contraindications.

Malignancies:

For patients with a malignancy, the required disease-free interval depends on the tumor type, stage, risk of recurrence, and transplant center. Metastases are an absolute contraindication.

Systemic diseases:

Advanced heart, lung, or liver insufficiency.

Positive crossmatch:

Detection in the recipient of circulating antibodies directed against donor antigens that are relevant to transplantation.

Indications for nephrectomy before transplantation:

Severe renal hypertension, chronic pyelonephritis (with or without VUR), substantial proteinuria, symptomatic or very large polycystic kidneys, recurrent nephrolithiasis, or kidney tumors suspected of being malignant.

Living-Donor Kidney Transplantation

Requirements for living kidney donation:

The recipient must be suitable for kidney transplantation. The donor must be related to or emotionally close to the recipient. Commercial donation must be excluded, as must psychological dependence of the donor on the recipient. Following the reform adopted in 2026, paired living-donor kidney exchange between different donor-recipient pairs will also be possible in Germany.

The donor should be in good health and have two healthy kidneys. See the following table for the pre-donation evaluation.

Protocols for intensified immunosuppression in ABO-incompatible kidney transplantation are also available. Regional paired-donation programs can enable living donation between ABO-incompatible donors and recipients; these will also be possible in Germany from 2026.

Risks of living kidney donation for the donor:

Mortality is 0.03–0.06 %, and serious complications occur in 2–4 %. Up to 30 % of donors experience complications, most commonly pneumonia, urinary tract infection, wound infection, pneumothorax, ileus, hematoma, incisional hernia, prolonged pain, thrombosis, and embolism (Kasiske et al., 1996). The laparoscopic surgical approach reduces the complication rate.

Donor nephrectomy technique:

Open, laparoscopic, or retroperitoneoscopic approach. The renal vessels are exposed, and the ureter is divided below the crossing of the vessels while preserving the periureteral vessels. The vessels are divided close to the great vessels, and the kidney is removed. The kidney is prepared and perfused ex situ with cold preservation solution.

Deceased-Donor Kidney Transplantation

Requirements for organ procurement:

Organ procurement may be considered after irreversible loss of all brain function has been established by two independent physicians. Consent for organ donation must also have been given by the patient (organ donation card) or the patient's relatives.

Donation after circulatory arrest:

In some countries (the United Kingdom, Switzerland, the Netherlands, Spain, Belgium, and the United States), circulatory arrest for 10 min at normal body temperature is sufficient for the final determination of death; brain function is then considered irreversibly lost. Successful kidney donation from non-heart-beating donors (NHBDs) is possible within 60–120 min, and long-term outcomes are comparable to those after donation following brain death. In Germany, organ transplantation after cardiac death is not permitted because of ethical concerns and the risk of misdiagnosis (Weiller et al., 2014).

Contraindications to organ procurement:

Malignancies capable of metastasis and transmissible infections (hepatitis, HIV, tuberculosis, or sepsis), as well as risk factors for transmissible infection such as drug abuse. Serum creatinine should be below 2 mg/dl.

Recipient selection:

Eurotransplant selects suitable recipients based on blood group, HLA characteristics, waiting time, immunological risk, recipient age, organ quality, and urgency. The aim is to distribute available organs fairly and in a medically appropriate manner. If the quality of a single kidney is considered inadequate, dual kidney transplantation may be performed, in which two marginal kidneys are transplanted together.

Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI):

The KDPI describes donor organ quality and correlates with graft survival. Factors used to calculate it include age, height, weight, ethnicity, arterial hypertension, diabetes, cause of death, serum creatinine, hepatitis C status, and donation after cardiac death. The KDPI is used for allocation in the United States: very good donor kidneys are allocated to patients with long life expectancy, while kidneys with a poor KDPI may still be a good option for older patients with comorbidities.

Donor kidney biopsy:

Donor kidney biopsy can assess organ quality before transplantation and is routinely performed in the United States. Indications include donor risk factors for kidney dysfunction, such as anuria, creatinine above 1.5 mg/dl, diabetes mellitus, KDPI above 85 %, or age above 60 years. Limitations include time pressure, inconsistent histological assessment, frozen-section artifacts, and the limited predictive value of individual findings. In the United States, donor kidney biopsy results are the most common reason for discarding a donor kidney.

Surgical Technique of Kidney Transplantation

Retroperitoneal access to the iliac vessels through a pararectal or Gibson incision. The lymphatic vessels are handled carefully. The left kidney is usually transplanted to the right iliac vessels and vice versa [Fig. kidney transplantation]. The vein is anastomosed before the artery. The common or external iliac artery and vein are usually used for end-to-side anastomoses. The ureter is implanted using an extravesical antirefluxing ureterocystoneostomy (Lich-Gregoir technique) with a short DJ stent.

Complications:

Hemorrhage, wound infection, arterial or venous graft thrombosis with loss of function, delayed graft function, lymphocele, urinoma, ureteral stenosis, vesicoureteral reflux, pyelonephritis, kidney stones, and hyperacute, acute, or chronic rejection (T-cell-mediated or antibody-mediated).

Immunosuppression After Kidney Transplantation

The following drug classes are used for immunosuppression after kidney transplantation:

  • Glucocorticoids
  • Calcineurin inhibitors: ciclosporin, tacrolimus
  • mTOR inhibitors: sirolimus, everolimus
  • IMPDH inhibitors: mycophenolate mofetil
  • Antimetabolites: azathioprine
  • Costimulation blockade: belatacept
  • Antibodies against immune cells: lymphocyte-depleting antibodies such as antithymocyte globulin or alemtuzumab; nondepleting antibodies such as basiliximab; rituximab in specific situations, particularly those involving B cells or antibody-mediated processes

Immunosuppression protocols:

Immunosuppressive therapy is divided into the following phases:

Induction therapy:

Immunosuppressive therapy before transplantation. This may include plasmapheresis to eliminate circulating antibodies and administration of antibodies against immune cells.

Initial therapy:

During the first months after kidney transplantation, combinations of tacrolimus or mycophenolate mofetil and a glucocorticoid are commonly used. Alternatives include individualized regimens with mTOR inhibitors, ciclosporin, or belatacept. Additional antibodies against immune cells are optional and are recommended for patients with delayed graft function or an increased risk of rejection (HLA antibodies).

Maintenance therapy:

Immunosuppressive drugs are then reduced. Regimens include calcineurin inhibitors, a second immunosuppressive agent such as azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil, and glucocorticoids. Rejection is treated with high-dose steroids or antibodies against immune cells.

Modifications:

Numerous modifications are available, including steroid-free or steroid-sparing regimens, calcineurin inhibitor minimization, mTOR-based regimens, and belatacept-based regimens. Selection depends on immunological risk, infection risk, tumor risk, adverse effects, kidney function, and the transplant center's experience.

Prognosis

Individual prognosis depends on donor and recipient age, organ quality, comorbidities, and immunological risk.

Living-donor kidney transplantation:

The graft function rate is approximately 95–98 % at 1 year and 70–80 % at 10 years.

Deceased-donor kidney transplantation:

The graft function rate is approximately 88–95 % at 1 year and 55–70 % at 10 years.
