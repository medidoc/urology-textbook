Overactive Bladder: Etiology, Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a clinically defined condition characterized by the storage symptoms of urinary urgency, increased daytime frequency, and nocturia, with or without urge incontinence, in the absence of an obvious local, metabolic, neurologic, or endocrine cause (Abrams et al., 2002). OAB without urge incontinence is called dry OAB; OAB with urge incontinence is called wet OAB. Other English synonyms include urge syndrome and urgency-frequency syndrome.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of OAB increases with age. Among adults older than 65 years, the prevalence is 22 % in men (10 % with urge incontinence) and 38 % in women (19 % with urge incontinence).

Etiology

Increased afferent signaling:

Increased triggering of urgency signals may result, among other factors, from activation of stretch receptors in the urothelium and adjacent connective tissue. This dysfunction may also be caused by detrusor hypertrophy (see BPH) or defects in the GAG layer (see also interstitial cystitis).

Decreased central inhibition:

Afferent urgency signals are normally inhibited in the CNS, including in the thalamus, thereby suppressing conscious perception of bladder filling. Decreased central inhibition leads to increased perception of bladder filling and thus to urgency symptoms.

Urinary bladder microbiome:

The prevailing view that the bladder and urine are sterile is being questioned in current research. Modern laboratory methods (e.g., PCR for bacterial DNA and enhanced quantitative urine culture [EQUC]) can detect bacteria in the urine of women, although conventional urine culture classifies these samples as sterile (Hilt et al., 2014). The significance of the urinary bladder microbiome is unclear, but there is evidence of a possible causal relationship with urgency symptoms (Pearce et al., 2014).

Signs and Symptoms

Frequency:

More than eight voids per 24 h in the presence of normal urine output (less than 2.8 L/24 h).

Urgency:

A sudden, compelling urge to urinate that is associated with a risk of urine leakage.

Nocturia:

Urgency and voiding during the night.

Urge incontinence:

Involuntary urine leakage associated with urgency.

Diagnosis

History and voiding diary:

A voiding diary helps objectively assess voiding symptoms. Recording fluid intake, incontinence episodes, pad changes, and voided volumes may also help quantify symptom severity and bother. Specific questions help with the differential diagnosis (previous operations, neurologic symptoms, medications, and bowel function).

Physical examination:

Examination of the genitalia, including assessment of the vaginal mucosa and measurement of vaginal pH; assessment of pelvic organ prolapse; rectal examination; assessment of prostate size; and a brief neurologic examination.

Urine sediment:

To rule out urinary tract infection or hematuria.

Imaging:

Ultrasonography of the kidneys and bladder, with the bladder full to assess detrusor thickness and after voiding to determine residual urine. If abnormalities are found, cystoscopy, voiding cystourethrography, retrograde pyelography, or excretory urography may be performed to rule out organic causes of voiding symptoms.

Cystoscopy or uroflowmetry:

To rule out subvesical obstruction and assess functional bladder capacity. Cystoscopy is more invasive but has greater diagnostic value, allowing bladder tumors, urethral disorders, and bladder stones to be ruled out.

Urodynamic studies:

Indicated when the diagnosis is unclear, there is significant residual urine, neurogenic dysfunction is suspected, treatment has failed, or invasive treatment is planned. Typical findings in patients with OAB include urgency beginning at a bladder volume of 100 mL, functional bladder capacity below 250 mL, and, sometimes, involuntary detrusor contractions.

Treatment of Overactive Bladder

Behavioral therapy:

Fluid intake and the frequency of bathroom visits can be optimized using the voiding diary to reduce symptoms. To a certain extent, functional bladder capacity can be increased by delaying voiding and suppressing urgency.

Pelvic floor muscle training with electrical stimulation:

Pelvic floor muscle training strengthens the sphincter and reduces urinary incontinence. Electrical stimulation via vaginal or anal electrodes, like sacral neuromodulation, inhibits the micturition reflex and relieves urgency symptoms.

Vaginal estrogen therapy:

Vaginal atrophy becomes clinically apparent several years after menopause. Subjective symptoms such as urgency and dyspareunia, or an elevated vaginal pH (> 5), occur in up to 50 % of postmenopausal women. Systemic hormone replacement therapy does not guarantee adequate local vaginal estrogenization; higher doses are often needed when estrogen is administered vaginally (Sturdee et al., 2010). Unless oncologic contraindications are present, vaginal estrogen therapy should be offered to every perimenopausal or postmenopausal woman with OAB. A Cochrane analysis found subjective cure and improvement rates for urge incontinence of 57 % with estrogen versus 28 % with placebo (Moehrer et al., 2003). Available formulations include tablets, vaginal suppositories or ovules, creams, and vaginal rings, which differ in their method of administration and associated discharge. Dose: 0.03–0.5 mg of intravaginal estriol once weekly.

Anticholinergic therapy:

Anticholinergics antagonize muscarinic receptors, causing relaxation of smooth muscle. In the bladder, they reduce the force of normal or autonomous detrusor contractions and increase functional bladder capacity.

A regular voiding schedule is needed for substantial symptom improvement. The goal is to void before bladder volume reaches the threshold that induces an autonomous contraction. The high rate of bothersome adverse effects often leads patients to discontinue treatment. The following agents are commonly used; see the section on anticholinergics for a detailed discussion of their pharmacology:

  • Trospium: up to 15 mg 1-1-1.
  • Oxybutynin: 2.5 mg 1-0-1 to 5 mg 1-1-1-1.
  • Tolterodine: 1 mg 1-0-0 to 2 mg 1-1-1. Extended-release formulation: 4 mg 1-0-0.
  • Fesoterodine: 4–8 mg 1-0-0.
  • Propiverine: 15 mg 1-0-1; may be increased to 1-1-1.
  • Darifenacin: 7.5–15 mg 1-0-0.
  • Solifenacin: 5–10 mg 1-0-0.

Adrenergic β3-receptor agonists:

Adrenergic β3 receptors are also present in the bladder, where they mediate detrusor relaxation. Selective β3-receptor agonists such as mirabegron and vibegron have proved to be treatment options with few adverse effects in clinical studies. Mirabegron is dosed at 50 mg 1-0-0 p. o.; reduce the dose to 25 mg 1-0-0 p. o. in severe renal or moderate hepatic insufficiency. Vibegron is dosed at 75 mg once daily.

Tibial nerve stimulation:

Stimulation of afferent fibers of the posterior tibial nerve modulates bladder function through sacral reflex pathways and may improve urgency, frequency, and urge incontinence in OAB. Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) uses a needle electrode; transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) uses surface electrodes. PTNS is typically administered weekly for about 12 weeks; TTNS can also be performed at home after instruction.

Botulinum toxin A:

Transurethral injections of botulinum toxin A into the detrusor cause temporary detrusor weakness lasting several months and increase functional bladder capacity. Botulinum toxin A is indicated when oral drug therapy fails or is not tolerated. Before injection, assess residual urine and the patient's willingness to perform intermittent self-catheterization if urinary retention occurs. The dose for OAB is 100 units of onabotulinumtoxinA (Botox). See the pharmacology section for further information about botulinum toxin.

Intravesical instillations:

Instillations with glycosaminoglycans, such as chondroitin sulfate, aim to regenerate the bladder GAG layer. In a small randomized study of patients with OAB, chondroitin sulfate instillations had better results than anticholinergic therapy (Gauruder et al., 2006). Overall, the therapeutic benefit of GAG instillations for OAB remains controversial.
