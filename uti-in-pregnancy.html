Urinary Tract Infections in Pregnancy

Epidemiology

The frequency of bacteriuria during pregnancy is approximately 4–7 %, comparable to the rate in women who are not pregnant. Approximately 20–30 % of pregnant women with bacteriuria develop acute pyelonephritis, especially during the third trimester acute pyelonephritis.

Etiology

  • Decreased peristalsis of the ureter.
  • Mechanical obstruction of the ureter by the uterus, especially on the right side.

Clinical Presentation of Urinary Tract Infections in Pregnancy

Acute cystitis:

The clinical presentation ranges from asymptomatic bacteriuria to severe dysuria, urinary frequency, and hematuria. See also acute cystitis.

Complications:

Urinary tract infection during pregnancy often leads to pyelonephritis. Pyelonephritis can be complicated by anemia (in 23 %), renal dysfunction (in 7 %), or respiratory failure (in 7 %). Urinary tract infections during pregnancy are also associated with preterm birth, reduced birth weight, increased neonatal mortality, and preeclampsia.

Diagnosis

Urine culture:

Midstream urine should be used in pregnant women; catheterization is avoided because of concern about causing an iatrogenic urinary tract infection. Screening with a urine culture is recommended at the first prenatal visit and between the 12th and 16th weeks of pregnancy. To avoid unnecessary antibiotic treatment, asymptomatic bacteriuria should be confirmed with a second urine sample. Although some authors question the need for urine cultures in asymptomatic women, screening is standard practice nationally. See also urine culture.

Ultrasound imaging:

Ultrasound imaging is used to rule out hydronephrosis or residual urine.

Laboratory tests:

Pyelonephritis causes systemic signs of inflammation, such as leukocytosis and elevated CRP. Obtain blood cultures in patients with fever.

Obstetric evaluation:

Depending on the symptoms, obstetric emergencies such as preterm labor should be ruled out.

Treatment of Urinary Tract Infections in Pregnancy

Asymptomatic bacteriuria and cystitis:

Any significant bacterial growth should be treated, which can significantly reduce the rate of pyelonephritis. Routine follow-up urine cultures after treatment are no longer recommended for all patients.

Antibiotics that can be used during pregnancy include pivmecillinam, oral amoxicillin (possibly with clavulanic acid), oral cephalosporins, and a single dose of fosfomycin. No significant differences in effectiveness or adverse effects are known. Treatment usually lasts 3–5 days.

Acute pyelonephritis in pregnancy:

Depending on the clinical presentation, hospitalization may be brief. Obstetric evaluation to rule out labor is advisable. First-line antibiotics are second-, third-, or fourth-generation cephalosporins, e.g., cefuroxime 1.5 g every 12 h, ceftriaxone 1–2 g every 24 h, or cefotaxime 1–2 g every 12 h i.v. Intravenous ampicillin is an option when urine culture confirms susceptibility. In life-threatening situations, the antibiotics listed above may be combined with intravenous gentamicin.
