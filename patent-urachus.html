Patent Urachus and Urachal Cysts: Diagnosis and Treatment

Urachal Anomalies

During embryonic development, the bladder primordium is connected to the allantois via the umbilical cord. Normally, the allantois obliterates, leaving the urachus as a fibrous cord along the anterior abdominal wall. Depending on the extent of failed regression, various urachal anomalies may persist [fig. urachal anomalies].


Urachal anomalies:
vesicourachal diverticulum (1)
urachal cyst (2)
umbilical fistula (urachal fistula) (3)

Epidemiology:

In children, the prevalence of sonographic abnormalities is approximately 1:500 (Keceli et al., 2021). The prevalence decreases with age, suggesting a high rate of spontaneous regression.

Patent urachus:

A patent urachus causes persistent urine leakage from the umbilicus after birth. Treatment consists of excision of the urachal fistula. Alternatively, conservative management with bladder catheterization and observation for spontaneous closure may be attempted (Lipskar et al., 2010).

Partially patent urachus:

If the bladder end of the urachus remains patent, a diverticulum arises from the bladder dome and may cause recurrent urinary tract infections or bladder stones. If the umbilical end remains patent, a urachal sinus develops and may cause scant discharge that is occasionally purulent [fig. MRI of a urachal fistula].


MRI of a urachal fistula (→). With kind permission, Prof. Dr. P. Haage and Dr. N. Dreger, Wuppertal.

Urachal cyst:

When the urachus obliterates at both the bladder and umbilical ends but remains patent between them, a cyst forms in the lower abdominal wall [fig. CT of an infected urachal cyst]. Rarely, it becomes symptomatic because of superinfection or presents as a palpable mass. Symptomatic urachal cysts may be treated by percutaneous drainage or surgical excision, including laparoscopic excision (Lipskar et al., 2010).


CT of an infected urachal cyst (*). With kind permission, Prof. Dr. K. Bohndorf, Augsburg.

Diagnosis:

Ultrasonography is the primary imaging modality. Depending on the findings, additional studies may include voiding cystourethrography (VCUG), MRI, CT, or cystoscopy.

Treatment:

Surgical treatment of urachal anomalies is necessary only when symptoms are present. The risk of urachal carcinoma is considered very low (1:5000); this does not justify prophylactic resection of asymptomatic urachal anomalies (Gleason et al., 2015).
