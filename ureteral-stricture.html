Ureteral Stricture: Etiology, Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition of Ureteral Stricture

Ureteral stricture is a scar-related, inflammatory, ischemic, or tumor-related narrowing of the ureter. It may cause hydronephrosis, recurrent infections, and unilateral loss of renal function.

Etiology of Ureteral Stricture

Ureteral diseases (intrinsic causes):

Ureteral stones, congenital disorders, infections (tuberculosis and schistosomiasis), idiopathic strictures, infiltrating endometriosis, and ureteral carcinoma.

Iatrogenic causes:

Endoscopic stone treatment, intraoperative ureteral injury, ureteral anastomoses, kidney transplantation, hysterectomy, colorectal surgery, vascular surgery, BCG instillations, and radiation therapy. Examples of procedures associated with injury include ureteroscopy, rectal surgery, hysterectomy, and vascular surgery. Iatrogenic ureteral injury is another cause of stricture.

Compression of the ureter (extrinsic causes):

For the differential diagnosis of hydronephrosis, see the section on upper urinary tract obstruction.

Signs and Symptoms of Ureteral Stricture

A slowly developing stricture is often asymptomatic and may first be detected because of hydronephrosis, infection, or worsening renal function. Other symptoms include flank pain, fever, and gross hematuria, depending on the underlying disease.

Diagnostic Workup

Basic workup:

Includes ultrasonography to assess hydronephrosis and renal parenchyma, urinalysis and urine culture, and serum creatinine; obtain a complete blood count and CRP if infection is suspected. In infected obstruction, prompt drainage takes priority over completion of the elective diagnostic workup.

CT or MR urography:

CT urography or MR urography shows the location, length, and cause of the stricture.

Renal scintigraphy:

Usually performed with MAG3, it assesses split renal function and identifies functionally significant obstruction. See renal scintigraphy.

Endoscopy:

Retrograde pyelography and ureterorenoscopy confirm the diagnosis and determine the stricture length. Ureteral biopsies and, when indicated, urine cytology are necessary if the etiology is unclear.

Treatment of Ureteral Stricture

Urinary diversion:

Initial drainage after endoscopic diagnosis is performed with a double-J ureteral stent or percutaneous nephrostomy. In reversible obstruction, a temporary double-J ureteral stent may be sufficient, for example, after endoscopic stone treatment, if urinary drainage and split renal function remain stable after stent removal.

Endoscopic balloon dilation:

After retrograde or antegrade pyelography and guidewire placement, the balloon dilator is advanced and inflated to a diameter of 4–6 mm. A double-J ureteral stent is left in place for 2–4 weeks. The success rate is 50–75 %. Balloon dilation is particularly suitable for short-segment strictures (< 2 cm) and strictures after ureteral stone lithotripsy. Recurrence rates are high for long, radiation-induced, ischemic, or recurrent strictures.

Endoscopic ureterotomy:

After retrograde or antegrade pyelography and guidewire placement, the ureteroscope is advanced to the stricture. The scar should be opened completely to the periureteral fat with a cold knife or holmium laser, taking care not to overlook an extrinsic or malignant cause. At or below the crossing of the iliac vessels, the ureter is incised anteromedially; above the crossing, it is incised lateroposteriorly to avoid major blood vessels. After ureterotomy, the largest-diameter double-J ureteral stent possible is left in place for 8–10 weeks. The success rate is slightly higher than that of balloon dilation; stricture length is the most important prognostic factor. See also vessels near the ureter.

Ureteroureterostomy:

Suitable for short-segment strictures of the proximal or mid ureter when a tension-free, well-vascularized end-to-end anastomosis is possible. For details, see ureteroureterostomy.

Ureteral reimplantation:

Ureteroneocystostomy is suitable for distal ureteral strictures. Short distal defects can be corrected by simple reimplantation. Longer defects can be bridged with a psoas hitch technique or a Boari flap. The length that can be replaced depends on bladder capacity and mobility, as well as kidney mobilization and caudal displacement.

Transureteroureterostomy:

An option for defects in the mid or distal ureter when a Boari flap is not feasible because of low bladder capacity or previous surgery. Contraindications include a very short donor ureter, disease of the contralateral ureter, retroperitoneal fibrosis (Ormond disease), prior radiation therapy, ureteral urothelial carcinoma, and recurrent nephrolithiasis. See transureteroureterostomy and recurrent nephrolithiasis.

Renal autotransplantation:

The kidney is explanted and anastomosed to the iliac vessels. The renal pelvis or a short residual ureter can then be anastomosed to the bladder. This is an option for complex proximal or long-segment defects when simpler reconstructions are not feasible and the kidney has salvageable function. See renal autotransplantation.

Ureteroplasty with a buccal mucosa graft:

A longitudinal incision is made in the stricture, and the defect is closed with a free buccal mucosa onlay graft. This technique is particularly suitable for longer benign strictures of the proximal or mid ureter, multifocal strictures, or recurrent strictures after pyeloplasty when the options described above are not suitable.

Ileal interposition:

Ileum can be used to reconstruct long-segment ureteral strictures, but this remains a salvage technique because of potential metabolic, infectious, and intestinal complications. A suitable ileal segment is brought into the retroperitoneum and anastomosed to the renal pelvis or proximal ureter and to the bladder. Isoperistaltic orientation supports urine transport. Contraindications include significant chronic kidney disease, bladder dysfunction, inflammatory bowel disease, and radiation injury to the bowel. Modified techniques to reduce complications and bowel length include tapering the ileal segment, using reconfigured ileum (Yang-Monti), and combining it with a Boari flap (Xiong et al., 2020).
