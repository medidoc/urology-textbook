Diagnosis and Treatment of Ureteral Injury and Ureter Trauma

Definition of Ureteral Injury

Ureteral injury is rare and usually results from penetrating trauma, such as gunshot or stab wounds. Less commonly, blunt deceleration trauma causes ureteral avulsion (Elliott and McAninch, 2003) (Elliott and McAninch, 2006). EAU Guidelines: Urological Trauma.

Left ureteral injury: Abdominal CT (delayed phase) and retrograde pyelography after a fall from a great height. Associated injuries included splenic rupture and a grade IV renal laceration. With kind permission (left image), Prof. Dr. K. Bohndorf, Augsburg.

Classification of Ureteral Injury

According to the AAST, bilateral ureteral injuries are assigned a grade one level higher:

  • Grade I: Hematoma without devascularization
  • Grade II: Laceration with less than 50 % transection
  • Grade III: Laceration with more than 50 % transection
  • Grade IV: Laceration with complete transection and less than 2 cm of devascularization
  • Grade V: Laceration or avulsion with more than 2 cm of devascularization

Signs and Symptoms of Ureteral Injury

The clinical presentation is usually dominated by associated injuries. Additional findings of ureteral injury may include a urinoma, abdominal pain, fever, urinary obstruction, elevated serum creatinine, gross hematuria, signs of peritoneal irritation, or urine leakage from a wound or drain.

Diagnostic Workup in Suspected Ureteral Injury

Laboratory tests include a complete blood count, serum creatinine, and C-reactive protein. Diagnostic imaging includes abdominal and renal ultrasonography and multiphase abdominal CT with an excretory phase. Intravenous urography has largely been replaced by CT. Findings include urinary obstruction or urine extravasation with urinoma.

When imaging findings are inconclusive or endoscopic treatment is being considered, cystoscopy, retrograde pyelography, antegrade pyelography, and ureteroscopy may be useful.

Treatment of Ureteral Injury

Minor ureteral injuries can often be managed endoscopically, usually by placement of a double-J ureteral stent and, if needed, percutaneous nephrostomy. Complete transection, avulsion, substantial devascularization, or failure of endoscopic drainage requires surgical reconstruction. Depending on the injury's location and defect length, options include primary ureteral repair, ureteroureterostomy, transureteroureterostomy, ureteral reimplantation, a psoas hitch, a Boari flap, or, rarely, more complex substitution procedures. In hemodynamically unstable trauma patients, initial urinary diversion as part of damage-control management followed by delayed reconstruction may be appropriate. Surgical reconstruction may also be required for a long-segment ureteral stricture.
