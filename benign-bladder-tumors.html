Benign Bladder Tumors: Diagnosis and Treatment

Benign tumors of the urinary bladder are rare. Lesions that can mimic bladder cancer on cystoscopy or imaging are of particular clinical importance.

Nonkeratinizing Squamous Metaplasia

This is a transformation of the urothelium in the region of the trigone into stratified, nonkeratinizing squamous epithelium. It is found in up to 40 % of asymptomatic women of reproductive age.

Etiology and pathogenesis:

Because the bladder trigone is embryologically derived from the urogenital sinus, its mucosa has a high density of estrogen receptors and, less commonly, progesterone receptors. Hormonal influences and recurrent infections can induce squamous metaplasia.

Cystoscopy:

Sharply demarcated, whitish, opaque, or fluffy plaques are confined to the trigone. Biopsy is not required when the location is typical and the history is unremarkable.

Cystitis Cystica

Cystitis cystica is an inflammatory reaction of the urothelium, with cyst-like elevations of the mucosa. See also ureteritis cystica .

Etiology and pathology:

This is an unclear inflammatory reaction of the urothelium to infection or mechanical irritation. Subepithelial vesicles a few millimeters in size develop. In the urinary bladder, cystitis cystica is most commonly found in the trigone.

Signs and symptoms:

Cystitis cystica is usually asymptomatic. Hematuria or symptoms of a urinary tract infection may occur.

Diagnosis:

Cystoscopy [ fig. cystitis cystica ]; biopsy of the lesion is generally not necessary.

Therapy:

Cystitis cystica itself does not require treatment. Improvement is expected after treating the underlying cause, for example with antibiotic therapy for a urinary tract infection.

Bladder Papilloma

Urothelial papilloma:

Urothelial papilloma is a rare exophytic tumor with delicate papillary structures and unremarkable urothelium. It usually occurs as a small, solitary tumor. The prognosis is excellent after complete transurethral resection, and recurrence is rare.

Inverted papilloma:

Inverted papilloma is a benign urothelial tumor with endophytic growth. Typical locations include the bladder neck and trigone. The lesion may grossly mimic urothelial carcinoma; therefore, complete transurethral resection with histologic confirmation is necessary.

Nephrogenic Adenoma

Nephrogenic adenoma is a rare, benign lesion that often appears as a papillary, cystic [ fig. Nephrogenic adenoma ], polypoid, or exophytic structure and may resemble papillary urothelial carcinoma.

Etiology:

The exact pathogenesis is unclear. One proposed mechanism is implantation of shed renal tubular cells onto damaged urothelium. Risk factors include long-term catheterization, bladder stones , prior surgery (TURBT or renal transplantation), and recurrent urinary tract infections.

Pathology:

Histologically, the tissue resembles renal tubules. Atypia is generally absent.

Therapy:

TURBT; recurrence is possible.

Inflammatory Pseudotumor

The inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm with intermediate biologic potential.

Clinical presentation and diagnosis:

The main symptoms are usually painless gross hematuria or suprapubic pain. Cystoscopy often shows a broad-based, reddish tumor that may grow infiltratively.

Histology:

There is a proliferation of spindle cells (myofibroblasts) in an edematous or myxoid stroma with a prominent inflammatory infiltrate of plasma cells and lymphocytes.

Differential diagnosis:

It is particularly important to exclude sarcomatoid carcinoma or rhabdomyosarcoma.

Therapy:

TURBT; recurrence is possible.

Leiomyoma of the Urinary Bladder

Leiomyoma is the most common benign mesenchymal tumor of the urinary bladder, although it is rare overall.

Pathology:

The tumor arises from the muscularis propria and grows as a well-circumscribed endovesical, intramural, or extravesical mass. Histologically, it resembles a uterine leiomyoma: long, spindle-shaped cells are arranged in whorled bundles, with no nuclear atypia or mitoses.

Cystoscopy:

There is a smooth, well-circumscribed bulge covered by intact, normal mucosa.

Therapy:

TURBT or partial cystectomy, depending on the size and location.

Hemangioma of the Urinary Bladder

Hemangiomas of the urinary bladder are rare benign vascular tumors. The leading symptom is painless gross hematuria. Cystoscopy shows bluish-red vascular lesions.

Pathology:

There are numerous vascular channels lined by endothelial cells. Hemosiderin deposits may also be present.

Therapy:

Small, typical lesions do not require biopsy. For atypical findings or active bleeding, biopsy, laser coagulation, or TURBT may be considered. Large lesions pose a significant risk of bleeding; imaging may be useful for treatment planning.

Endometriosis of the Urinary Bladder

Bladder endometriosis is a benign tumor-like lesion consisting of endometrial tissue in the bladder wall. Typical symptoms include cyclic dysuria, suprapubic pain, urinary frequency, or hematuria.

Cystoscopy:

Bluish-red or chocolate-colored lesions may be seen beneath the mucosa and are particularly visible before menstruation. Endometriosis infiltrates the bladder from the outside inward; bladder wall involvement is often not visible on cystoscopy.

Malakoplakia of the Urinary Bladder

Malakoplakia is a rare chronic granulomatous inflammation that may appear on cystoscopy as yellowish plaques or tumor-like lesions.

Fibroepithelial Polyp

A fibroepithelial polyp (FEP) is a benign tumor with a fibrovascular stroma and urothelial covering. It occurs predominantly in young adults and is usually found in the proximal ureter, but it can also occur in the urinary bladder. Grossly, it appears as an elongated, finger-like, or “seaweed-like” structure that can grow several centimeters long and usually has a smooth surface. When it occurs in the urinary bladder, TURBT is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

Keratinizing Squamous Metaplasia

Keratinizing squamous metaplasia is a pathologic transformation of the urothelium into keratinizing squamous epithelium in response to chronic cystitis.

Etiology:

The lesion usually develops in response to chronic mucosal irritation, for example from recurrent infections, stones, catheterization, or bladder-emptying dysfunction. It is considered a potential precursor to squamous cell carcinoma.

Pathology:

Histology shows stratified squamous epithelium with formation of a stratum corneum (keratinization). Dysplasia must be ruled out.

Cystoscopy:

Whitish to gray, scaly plaques are seen. Debris, blood deposits, or encrustations may also cause dark discoloration [ fig. Keratinizing Squamous Metaplasia ].

Therapy:

The underlying chronic cystitis should be treated, for example by eradicating infection or eliminating residual urine. TURBT or fulguration of the affected areas may be considered. Because of the potential risk of subsequent bladder squamous cell carcinoma, cystoscopic surveillance is necessary.
