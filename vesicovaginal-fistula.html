Vesicovaginal Fistula: Etiology, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Definition

Definition:

A vesicovaginal fistula is an abnormal connection between the urinary bladder and the vagina that causes urinary incontinence.

Etiology:

Hysterectomy (incidence 0.1–2 %, depending on the indication). Pelvic tumors (due to infiltration, radiation therapy, or surgery). Trauma. Obstetric surgery or prolonged labor with pressure necrosis of the anterior vaginal wall and bladder neck (the most common cause in developing countries).

Signs and symptoms of a vesicovaginal fistula:

Continuous urinary incontinence, day and night; passage of urine through the vagina; and recurrent urinary tract infections.

Diagnosis

Pelvic examination:

After indigo carmine is instilled into the urinary bladder, a vaginally inserted swab turns blue.

Cystoscopy:

Cystoscopy with biopsy (see figure below) and, if indicated, retrograde pyelography to rule out ureteral involvement.

Imaging:

Cystography and, if indicated, abdominal CT to rule out malignancy or ureteral involvement.

Therapy of Vesicovaginal Fistula

Conservative treatment:

Newly diagnosed vesicovaginal fistulas after gynecologic surgery may heal with indwelling catheter drainage for 3–4 weeks (success rate 10–50 %). The outcome is difficult to predict in individual cases. Favorable prognostic factors include early diagnosis and treatment within seven days, a small fistula measuring less than 1 cm, no urine leakage while the catheter is in place, and no previous radiation therapy or cancer surgery.

Timing of surgical treatment:

The delay between fistula formation and surgical treatment is very distressing for the patient. Simple fistulas caused by injury during pelvic surgery can be repaired without delay. For fistulas after prolonged labor, repair should be delayed for three months to allow inflammatory and necrotic tissue changes to heal. Local vaginal estrogen therapy is recommended before surgery in postmenopausal women. Some authors recommend delaying repair of radiation-induced fistulas for up to one year.

Vaginal approach:

Indicated for smaller, uncomplicated fistulas. The advantage of the vaginal approach is lower postoperative morbidity.

Technique:

Circumferential incision around the fistula tract and dissection of the anterior vaginal wall. It is important to separate the bladder wall from the vaginal wall well beyond the fistula defect. The bladder is closed in two layers (urothelium and tunica muscularis). If necessary, a peritoneal flap or a Martius flap made from labial fat is prepared to cover the defect. The anterior vaginal wall is closed. Cystography is performed after 14 days, followed by removal of the indwelling catheter.

Abdominal approach:

The abdominal approach is more extensive and more burdensome for the patient. It is indicated for fistulas larger than 2 cm, after radiation therapy, for recurrent fistulas, for combined bladder-ureter-bowel-vaginal fistulas, or when the vagina is narrow. In experienced hands, laparoscopic or robot-assisted surgery offers advantages in access-related morbidity and visualization of the operative field; it is also an alternative to the vaginal approach.

Technique:
  • Temporary ureteral stenting.
  • Lower abdominal laparotomy or laparoscopic access.
  • A sagittal incision is made in the posterior bladder wall down to the fistula, and the fistula tract is excised.
  • The bladder wall is separated from the vaginal wall well beyond the fistula defect; the ureteral stents help with orientation.
  • The vagina is closed transversely in one layer.
  • A peritoneal or omental flap is prepared to cover the defect.
  • The bladder is closed sagittally in two layers.
  • A drain is placed, and the wound is closed.
  • Cystography is performed after 14 days, followed by removal of the indwelling catheter.

Palliative cystectomy:

Palliative cystectomy is indicated for vesicovaginal fistulas caused by advanced malignancy or a contracted bladder after radiation therapy.
