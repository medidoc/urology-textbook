Ectopic Ureter: Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition of Ectopic Ureter

An ectopic ureter is a congenital abnormal insertion of the ureter at the bladder neck, in the urethra, or outside the urinary tract. The ectopic ureter may drain the upper pole of a duplex kidney or a single renal system (EAU guidelines).

Epidemiology

The prevalence is approximately 1:2000; ectopic ureters are more common in girls than in boys.

Etiology

See the etiology of ureteral duplication. An ectopic ureter results from abnormal development of the ureteral bud and its insertion into the urogenital sinus. Complete ureteral duplication is common.

Pathology of Ectopic Ureter

Approximately 80 % of ectopic ureters are associated with a duplex kidney. This is especially common in girls; in boys, an ectopic ureter more often drains a single renal system.

In girls, the ectopic ureter drains into the urethra (35 %), vulval vestibule (34 %), or vagina (25 %), and rarely into the uterus or fallopian tubes. In boys, it drains into the posterior urethra (47 %), seminal vesicles (33 %), prostatic utricle (10 %), or vas deferens (10 %).

The farther the ectopic ureteral orifice is from the trigone, the greater the likelihood of renal malformation (dysplasia or hypoplasia) and reduced function of the corresponding renal segment. In a duplex kidney, the malformation affects the upper pole.

Signs and Symptoms of an Ectopic Ureter

Symptoms include recurrent urinary tract infections, flank pain, hydronephrosis, febrile pyelonephritis, and a risk of arterial hypertension. Additional symptoms depend on the sex and location of the ectopic orifice:

  • Girls: urinary incontinence (day and night, sometimes intermittent) and vaginal discharge.
  • Boys: voiding symptoms and epididymitis. Incontinence is atypical because the ureteral orifice is located above the external sphincter.

Diagnosis of Ureteral Ectopia

Pelvic examination:

Pelvic examination is indicated in girls with incontinence. The ectopic ureteral orifice can sometimes be identified near the urethral meatus or in the vulval vestibule.

Cystoscopy and retrograde pyelography:

Cystoscopy and retrograde pyelography are used to locate the ureteral orifices and define the anatomy, particularly when cross-sectional imaging is inconclusive or surgery is planned.

Ultrasound:

In a duplex kidney, obstruction is generally limited to the upper moiety. A dilated ureter may sometimes be seen behind the bladder.

CT or MR urography:

CT or MR urography is indicated when ultrasound findings are abnormal and to confirm the suspected diagnosis. MR urography is preferred in children and for elective imaging because it avoids radiation exposure. CT is particularly useful when a stone, acute symptoms, or a tumor is suspected, when MRI is unavailable, or when MRI is contraindicated. See also CT and MRI.

Intravenous urography:

Intravenous urography has largely been replaced by cross-sectional imaging. The upper renal moiety is often not visualized because of poor function, but it may opacify on delayed images. Signs of a nonopacified upper moiety include a reduced number of visible calyces, caudal displacement of the lower pole calyces (dropping lily sign), and an increased distance between the renal moiety and the spine.

Voiding cystourethrography:

Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) is indicated in patients with recurrent febrile urinary tract infections and suspected vesicoureteral reflux. In a duplex kidney, reflux usually affects the lower moiety.

Renal scintigraphy:

Renal scintigraphy is used to assess the function and urinary drainage of the renal segment drained by the ectopic ureter.

Treatment of Ectopic Ureter

Treatment is generally necessary only in patients with symptoms, urinary incontinence, recurrent urinary tract infections, clinically significant vesicoureteral reflux, or significant obstruction.

Ectopic ureter with a salvageable renal segment:

  • Ureteropyelostomy or ureteroureterostomy for a duplex kidney. Usually, the ectopic upper pole ureter is anastomosed end-to-side to the lower pole ureter. If clinically significant reflux into the ectopic ureter is present, excision of the distal ectopic ureteral stump through a second approach may be necessary.
  • Common sheath ureteroneocystostomy (UCN) for a duplex kidney with closely spaced ureteral orifices and reflux into one or both ureters.
  • Ureteroneocystostomy (UCN) when the ureter drains a single renal system.

Ectopic ureter with a poorly functioning renal segment:

For a duplex kidney, heminephrectomy with partial ureterectomy may be considered. Complex dissection at the renal hilum carries a small risk of loss of the remaining kidney. Nephrectomy is the standard operation for a poorly functioning single renal system. These procedures can be performed laparoscopically or with robotic assistance. If reflux into the ectopic ureter is present, ureterectomy is also necessary; ureteral reimplantation may be required for reflux into the lower moiety of a duplex kidney.

Alternatively, ureteral anastomosis can be considered for an ectopic ureter in a duplex kidney with poor function of the affected moiety (end-to-side ureteroureterostomy or ureteropyelostomy of the upper pole ureter to the lower pole system). This approach has fewer complications and poses less risk to the remaining kidney. Retrospective data show comparable outcomes for postoperative urinary tract infections and hypertension (Kawal et al., 2019).
