Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction: Etiology and Symptoms

Neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD) comprises dysfunction of the bladder, bladder neck, urethra, and/or sphincter apparatus caused by diseases or injuries of the central nervous system, spinal cord, or peripheral nerves involved in urine storage and emptying. See the EAU Guidelines on Neuro-urology.

Classification of Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction

Simplified classification

Lower urinary tract function is divided into the storage and emptying phases. Neurogenic bladder dysfunction during either phase is caused by a disorder of bladder function, urethral function, or a combination of both.

International Continence Society

Lower urinary tract function is divided into the storage and emptying phases; urodynamic findings provide further classification. See Table 1.

Clinically Defined Disorders

Overactive bladder (OAB)

The syndrome comprises the storage symptoms of urinary urgency, frequency, and nocturia, with or without urgency incontinence, in the absence of an obvious local, metabolic, neurological, or endocrine cause (Abrams et al., 2002). Other English synonyms include urge syndrome and urgency-frequency syndrome. See the overactive bladder section for details.

Urgency urinary incontinence

Urgency urinary incontinence is involuntary urine loss accompanied by or immediately preceded by urinary urgency.

Stress urinary incontinence

An increase in abdominal pressure raises bladder pressure above the urethral closure pressure, which is reduced by neurological disease, causing urinary incontinence during physical exertion.

Urodynamically Defined Disorders

The following disorders can only be distinguished by urodynamic testing; see also Table 1.

Detrusor overactivity

Detrusor overactivity (DO) may be phasic (an increase in detrusor pressure during bladder filling without complete bladder emptying) or terminal (a detrusor contraction during bladder filling that results in complete bladder emptying). When DO is attributable to a neurological disease, it is termed neurogenic DO (formerly detrusor hyperreflexia). In idiopathic DO, the cause remains unclear (formerly detrusor instability).

Detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD)

DSD is defined as a detrusor contraction accompanied by simultaneous involuntary pelvic floor activation that can be attributed to a neurological disease (Figure 1). Classic DSD occurs with lesions between the pontine and sacral micturition centers. DSD causes high bladder emptying pressures. Without consistent treatment, complications of the upper urinary tract may develop.

Dysfunctional voiding

Dysfunctional voiding is characterized by an intermittent or fluctuating urinary stream due to intermittent pelvic floor activity on electromyography (EMG) in patients without a recognizable neurological disease. Alternative terms include idiopathic detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia and detrusor-sphincter dyscoordination.

Epidemiology

Neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction is uncommon in children. Common causes include spina bifida, spinal cord injury, cerebral palsy, and anorectal malformations. Its frequency increases with age, reflecting the epidemiology of the underlying neurological diseases.

Etiology, Pathogenesis, and Clinical Features

General principles

Lesions above the brainstem cause detrusor overactivity of varying severity. Sphincter control is usually preserved.

Complete spinal cord injuries below Th6 initially cause spinal shock with an atonic bladder, followed by a hyperreflexic bladder with detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia involving the striated sphincter. Injuries above Th6 may additionally cause smooth muscle sphincter dyssynergia and autonomic hyperreflexia.

Sacral lesions and lesions of the peripheral reflex arc cause a hypocontractile detrusor, increased bladder capacity, and variable residual sphincter activity without dyssynergia.

Stroke

Acute stroke often causes a hypocontractile detrusor with a risk of urinary retention. Detrusor overactivity with preserved sphincter function develops after several weeks to months. The long-term prevalence of incontinence after stroke is 20–30 %. Causes include detrusor overactivity, reduced control of the striated sphincter, and reduced perception of bladder filling.

Traumatic brain injury

The clinical features are similar to those after stroke. Brainstem lesions may also cause DSD.

Dementia

In patients with incontinence, distinguish intellectual deficits causing voiding dysfunction from detrusor overactivity. Anticholinergics are contraindicated in Alzheimer disease.

Parkinson disease

Voiding disorders occur in 35–70 % of patients, usually as detrusor overactivity with urgency and incontinence. Impaired sphincter control and detrusor underactivity may also occur; these can complicate surgical treatment of bladder outlet obstruction in patients with Parkinson disease.

Multiple system atrophy

Shy-Drager syndrome and olivopontocerebellar atrophy are neurodegenerative diseases with extrapyramidal, cerebellar, pyramidal, and autonomic deficits. Voiding symptoms such as incontinence and urgency may precede diagnosis by years.

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

A chronic relapsing or progressive demyelinating disease of the central nervous system of unknown etiology, with ataxia, nystagmus, paralysis, and bladder dysfunction (50–90 %). Voiding symptoms are the first manifestation of MS in 10 % of patients, usually as urinary retention or urgency. Depending on lesion location, urodynamics may show detrusor overactivity (30–90 %), DSD (30–65 %), or a hypocontractile detrusor (5–20 %).

Spinal cord injury

Acute spinal cord injury causes flaccid paresis of affected muscle groups and sensory loss in dermatomes below the lesion. Peripheral vasodilation may also occur, progressing to spinal shock. Acute injury causes an acontractile detrusor with urinary retention; weakness of the striated urinary sphincter is compensated for by the intact smooth muscle sphincter. After the hypocontractile phase, neurogenic detrusor overactivity develops after 6–12 weeks (see below). With an incomplete lesion, the phase of detrusor weakness may last only a few days.

Lesions of the spinal cord above the sacral micturition center

Causes include trauma above L1, multiple sclerosis, infarction, infection, and tumors. The sacral micturition reflex remains intact, but pontine and cortical inhibition is lost. Loss of central inhibition causes neurogenic detrusor overactivity and detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia. The resulting bladder may have reduced functional capacity, high intravesical voiding pressures, bladder wall hypertrophy, pelvic floor spasticity, and a risk of upper urinary tract damage from reflux and hydronephrosis. The severity of detrusor overactivity and DSD varies with the location and extent of the neurological lesion.

Autonomic dysreflexia

A potentially life-threatening emergency in patients with spinal cord injury, usually with lesions above Th6. Urinary tract manipulation, urinary retention, urinary tract infection, or catheter problems may trigger autonomic dysreflexia. Symptoms include bradycardia, hypertensive crises, headache, piloerection, and sweating. Autonomic dysreflexia with hypertensive crisis requires emergency treatment. Prophylaxis with 10–20 mg of sublingual nifedipine is possible. In rare, severe cases, sacral deafferentation may be required as salvage therapy.

Lesions of the sacral spinal cord

Causes include trauma below L1, polio or herpes zoster infection, herniated disks, radiation, surgery, tumors, infarction, or spina bifida. Sacral spinal cord lesions cause a hypocontractile or acontractile detrusor. Because complete lesions are uncommon, mixed patterns of detrusor overactivity and weakness often occur. External sphincter and pelvic floor tone are reduced. Urinary incontinence is not inevitable because the bladder may enlarge compensatorily.

Spina bifida

Spina bifida is a congenital neural tube defect involving incomplete closure of the spine, with possible damage to sacral spinal cord segments and nerve roots. Urodynamics often show mixed findings, including detrusor overactivity, DSD, detrusor underactivity, and sphincter insufficiency. Worsening bladder function over time may indicate tethered cord syndrome.

Cauda equina syndrome

A clinical syndrome characterized by saddle anesthesia, erectile dysfunction, and loss of voluntary control of the anal and urinary sphincters due to cauda equina injury.

Lesions of afferent innervation

Causes include diabetes mellitus, syphilis (tabes dorsalis), pernicious anemia, genital herpes, and spinal cord dorsal horn lesions. Bladder filling is not perceived, resulting in bladder overdistension, secondary myogenic detrusor weakness, increased bladder capacity, and residual urine.

Diabetes mellitus

Peripheral neuropathy impairs afferent and efferent innervation of the lower urinary tract. Bladder capacity gradually increases and voiding intervals lengthen. Over time, the overdistended bladder may decompensate, causing urinary retention. Timely diagnosis and regular voiding can often prevent this complication.

Lesions of the peripheral nerves

Causes include anterior rectal resection, Wertheim hysterectomy, pelvic fractures, and radiation. Loss of the sacral micturition reflex causes a hypo- or areflexic bladder, often with urinary retention despite preserved residual sphincter function. A storage-phase disorder may also be present.
