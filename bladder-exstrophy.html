Bladder Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex

Definition of Bladder Exstrophy and Epispadias

The bladder exstrophy-epispadias complex (BEEC) is a rare malformation syndrome involving severe defects of the bladder (bladder exstrophy), urethra (epispadias), and/or intestine (cloacal exstrophy), often with lifelong consequences (Kiddoo et al., 2004).




Epidemiology

The incidence is approximately 2–3 per 100,000 births. Boys are affected approximately two to three times more often than girls.

Pathology of the Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex

The individual components of BEEC vary considerably in their severity.

Bladder exstrophy:

The anterior abdominal wall fails to close, leaving the bladder open anteriorly and the trigone directly visible. The bladder mucosa prolapses through the abdominal wall defect. The symphysis is also diastatic, with separation of the right and left pubic bones (distance 2–8 cm).

Epispadias:

Epispadias is a dorsal cleft of the urethra that varies in extent. In boys, the urethral meatus is displaced dorsally, and the penis is shortened and deformed. In girls, the clitoris is often bifid and the urethra is open. The more proximal the defect, the more likely it is to involve the bladder neck and sphincter mechanism and cause urinary incontinence.

Cloacal exstrophy:

This malformation involves failure of the ventral colon wall to close. The ileum opens proximally and the anus distally. A portion of the bladder, with one ureteral orifice, lies on each side of the colon, which is open anteriorly.

Common associated anomalies:

Spinal and pelvic deformities, hip dysplasia, upper urinary tract anomalies, uterine duplication, inguinal hernias, and congenital heart defects.

Etiology of Bladder Exstrophy and Epispadias

Genetics:

BEEC is familial and is probably caused by polygenic factors, with environmental factors such as smoking and hormonal changes also contributing. The risk to siblings is 0.3–2.3 %.

Embryology:

Failure of mesoderm to grow into the cloacal membrane prevents development of the anterior abdominal wall. The fragile cloacal membrane ruptures during embryonic development. The timing and developmental stage at rupture determine the extent of the malformation (bladder exstrophy, epispadias, or cloacal exstrophy). Alternative explanations include defects in the development of the genital tubercle, pelvic bones, or lateral body wall folds.

Diagnosis

Prenatal diagnosis:

On high-resolution screening ultrasound, suspected bladder exstrophy is indicated by failure to visualize the fetal bladder despite normal amniotic fluid volume, a low insertion of the umbilical cord, and widening of the pubic symphysis.

Postnatal diagnosis:

Clinical examination of the size and elasticity of the bladder plate can help estimate future bladder capacity.

Ultrasound imaging:

Examine the upper urinary tract with renal ultrasound to rule out hydronephrosis or other anomalies.

Pelvic radiograph:

Precisely quantify symphyseal diastasis to help plan a possible pelvic osteotomy.

Echocardiography:

Before primary closure, rule out commonly associated cardiac anomalies.

Treatment of the Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex

Initial Management After Birth

  • Ligate the umbilical cord; avoid mechanical clamps that could injure the bladder plate.
  • Protect the delicate bladder mucosa with sterile plastic wrap and moisten it regularly with saline.
  • Transfer the child to a pediatric urology center experienced in the surgical management of this complex malformation.

Primary Closure of Bladder Exstrophy

For successful primary closure, the estimated bladder capacity should be greater than 5 ml. Surgery is performed during the first days of life and includes complete mobilization of the bladder plate, preparation of the urethral plate and corpora, closure of the bladder with the bladder neck and proximal urethra, and approximation of the symphysis over the bladder neck. Some authors advocate closing the epispadias during the same procedure when conditions are favorable.


Schematic illustration of the surgical steps for epispadias repair.

Epispadias Repair

Repair is usually performed at 6–12 months of age. Preoperative testosterone may be used to increase phallic size. The procedure includes preparation and tubularization of the urethral plate, separation of the corpora cavernosa ("penile disassembly technique"), relocation of the neourethra to the ventral aspect of the penis, reunification of the corpora, and skin closure.

Surgical Alternatives for Low Bladder Capacity

  • Delayed closure (6–12 months after birth), allowing the bladder plate to grow.
  • Closure with augmentation cystoplasty using ileum.
  • Urinary diversion with a catheterizable continent stoma.

Prognosis

Long-term prognosis depends largely on continence, preservation of renal function, and sexual function. Many patients require additional reconstructive procedures over time. Fertility disorders are more common in men, while women can generally become pregnant.
