Diagnosis and Treatment of Bladder Injury

Definition and Epidemiology of Bladder Injury

Bladder injuries commonly occur in association with pelvic fractures. Less common mechanisms include penetrating trauma and iatrogenic injury during surgery (Machtens et al., 2000). See the EAU Guidelines on Urological Trauma.

AAST Classification of Bladder Injury

The American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) classifies bladder injuries as follows:

Grade I:

Bladder contusion, intramural hematoma, or partial-thickness laceration.

Grade II:

Extraperitoneal bladder wall laceration <2 cm.

Grade III:

Extraperitoneal bladder wall laceration >2 cm or intraperitoneal bladder wall laceration <2 cm.

Grade IV:

Intraperitoneal bladder wall laceration >2 cm.

Grade V:

Bladder injury extending into the trigone or bladder neck/sphincter region.

Etiology

Pelvic fracture:

Bladder injury associated with a pelvic fracture is usually caused by shearing forces and, less commonly, by perforation from sharp fracture fragments. The injury is predominantly extraperitoneal.

Bladder rupture:

Blunt force to the lower abdomen can cause a sudden increase in intravesical pressure and bladder rupture when the bladder is full, as may occur in motor vehicle collisions. The rupture is usually intraperitoneal and located at the bladder dome.

Penetrating injury:

Stab or gunshot wounds may cause intraperitoneal bladder injury and are often associated with bowel or vascular injuries.

Iatrogenic injury:

Bladder injury may occur during pelvic or transurethral surgery, including cesarean delivery, vaginal hysterectomy, retropubic sling placement, and transurethral resection of bladder tumors.

Signs and Symptoms

Pelvic fracture:

Pelvic pain, instability on compression of the iliac wings, shock, gluteal hematomas, and lower abdominal tenderness.

Bladder injury:

Macroscopic hematuria, absent or reduced urine output, lower abdominal pain, peritonitis, and increasing retention parameters due to reabsorption of urinary creatinine.

Diagnosis

CT cystography with retrograde filling of the bladder with contrast medium is the diagnostic procedure of choice. It also allows assessment of associated injuries. If urethral injury is suspected, perform retrograde urethrography before catheterization. If CT is unavailable, obtain conventional pelvic radiographs, including images before and after retrograde filling of the bladder with contrast medium.

Treatment

Conservative treatment:

Uncomplicated extraperitoneal bladder ruptures can be managed with transurethral catheter drainage. Before choosing conservative treatment, exclude bone fragments protruding into the bladder, bladder neck injury, and associated rectal or vaginal injuries. Perform follow-up cystography before removing the catheter.

Surgical treatment:

Complex extraperitoneal and all intraperitoneal bladder ruptures require surgical repair. For an isolated bladder injury, a lower midline laparotomy provides access; laparoscopic repair is an option in selected cases. Inspect the abdominal cavity for associated injuries. Close the bladder injury watertight in multiple layers with absorbable sutures.
