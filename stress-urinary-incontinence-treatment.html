Treatment of Stress Urinary Incontinence (SUI)

Conservative Treatment

Initial measures include pelvic floor muscle training (Kegel exercises, with vaginal cones or biofeedback if appropriate), weight loss for patients with obesity, regular voiding, and vaginal estrogen therapy for postmenopausal vaginal atrophy.

Pharmacologic Treatment of Stress Urinary Incontinence

Pharmacologic treatment has a limited role in pure SUI. Duloxetine can reduce incontinence episodes but should be considered only after an individualized risk-benefit assessment because of adverse effects and treatment discontinuation. For mixed urinary incontinence with coexisting sensory or motor detrusor overactivity, treatment of the urgency component takes priority [see overactive bladder].

Duloxetine

Duloxetine is a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor that enhances urethral sphincter activity at the spinal level. It can reduce incontinence episodes by 50–60 %, compared with 20–40 % with placebo (Mariappan et al., 2007). Nausea, dry mouth, fatigue, and treatment discontinuation are common. For dosage and adverse effects, see the section on duloxetine. Because of safety concerns, duloxetine is not approved in the United States for the treatment of SUI.

Electromagnetic stimulation of the pelvic floor

An electromagnetic chair stimulates the perineal nerves, causing pelvic floor contraction. This treatment is used for SUI, overactive bladder, and mixed incontinence. Randomized trials show, at most, small and probably temporary effects (Gilling et al., 2009; Quek, 2005).

Surgical Treatment of SUI

Periurethral injections

Periurethral bulking agents, such as collagen, silicone, polydimethylsiloxane, polyacrylamide hydrogel, or autologous fat, can be used. Long-term cure rates are lower than those after sling procedures, but morbidity is lower. Periurethral injections may be considered for patients who prefer minimally invasive treatment, have an elevated surgical risk, or have recurrent incontinence.

Suprapubic procedures

Burch colposuspension has high long-term success rates and was formerly the gold standard of surgical treatment. Its use has declined substantially in favor of vaginal and minimally invasive procedures.

Burch colposuspension

The paravaginal tissue is secured to the iliopectineal ligament (Cooper ligament) with mattress sutures. The procedure can be performed through an open or laparoscopic approach. Laparoscopy usually allows faster recovery, although the evidence on continence outcomes and complications is heterogeneous.

Abdominal sacrocolpopexy

Open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted sacrocolpopexy is used to repair uterine or posthysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse. The Y-shaped mesh extends into the anterior and posterior compartments to correct the corresponding defects. The benefits and risks of synthetic mesh must be discussed individually; its safety remains controversial.

Vaginal procedures

Alloplastic vaginal slings are among the most common surgical procedures for SUI. Patients require detailed counseling about benefits, alternatives, and potential mesh-related complications. For details of sling procedures and complications, see midurethral slings.

Alloplastic anterior vaginal slings

The surgical principle is similar to that of a fascial sling. Through a small incision in the anterior vaginal wall, the urethra is dissected. A 1 cm-wide sling is placed tension-free around the urethra and exits suprapubically [TVT sling] or through the obturator foramen [transobturator sling]. Tissue ingrowth stabilizes the sling and restores pressure transmission to the urethra as abdominal pressure rises. For details of the procedures and complications, see the section on midurethral slings.

Suprapubic alloplastic anterior vaginal slings [figure] were first described as tension-free vaginal tape (TVT). Randomized trials showed 5-year outcomes comparable to those of Burch colposuspension (Ward et al., 2008). Several manufacturers are now on the market; the slings differ in material and surgical technique.

Transobturator slings are known as TOT (transobturator tape) [figure]. Randomized trials and meta-analyses show comparable initial outcomes for TOT and TVT. In the long term, retropubic slings have advantages in objective cure rates, while complication profiles differ by procedure (Alexandridis et al., 2023).

Minimally invasive alternatives include short slings (single-incision slings), which are anchored in the paraurethral tissue and pelvic floor muscles through a vaginal incision alone [figure]. Current controlled trials show comparable medium-term efficacy in selected patients, but higher rates of mesh erosion, dyspareunia, and reoperation (Abdel-Fattah et al., 2022).

Pubovaginal fascial sling

The pubovaginal fascial sling became less common after the introduction of alloplastic vaginal slings but is gaining renewed interest because of concerns about synthetic materials. After a Pfannenstiel incision, autologous fascia is harvested from the rectus sheath (1.5 × 10 cm). Through a vaginal incision, the urethra is dissected as for TVT. A strong suture is attached to each end of the fascial graft, and both ends are passed from the vagina to the suprapubic area with a Stamey needle. The graft is positioned like a TVT sling. It is secured by tying the two suture ends suprapubically above the rectus sheath. Compared with alloplastic slings, the autologous fascial sling has somewhat lower cure rates for urinary incontinence, but it avoids complications from nonabsorbable foreign material. Harvesting fascia through an open incision significantly increases operative and recovery time.

Vaginal surgery for pelvic floor insufficiency

The incontinence procedures described above can be combined with corrective surgery for pelvic floor insufficiency.

Risks of pelvic floor implants

Complications such as chronic pain, dyspareunia in up to 10 %, sling erosion (up to 12 %), infection, and difficulty removing mesh have led to warnings from several national regulatory authorities and withdrawal of some products from the market. In some countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia, transvaginal mesh for genital prolapse surgery has been prohibited. This has also had a substantial effect on the use of vaginal slings for SUI.

The current German guideline for pelvic floor insufficiency, the European Commission SCENIHR recommendations, and EAU guidelines allow pelvic floor mesh only for defined indications. Patients must be carefully counseled about benefits, risks, alternatives, and possible revision surgery.

  • Synthetic midurethral slings are established and recommended procedures for SUI when indicated according to current guidelines.
  • Single-incision slings may be used in selected patients. Patients should be counseled that long-term evidence is more limited than for conventional midurethral slings.
  • Sacrocolpopexy with abdominal mesh is an established procedure for correcting uterine or vaginal vault prolapse, provided that the indication, surgical technique, and counseling follow current guidelines.
  • Transvaginal mesh for genital prolapse surgery is not appropriate for primary repair and should be considered only for complex recurrent prolapse in experienced centers after careful assessment.
