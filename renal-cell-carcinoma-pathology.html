Pathology and TNM Stages of Renal Cell Carcinoma

  • Renal cell carcinoma: Targeted therapy of advanced disease

TNM Tumor Stages [UICC 2025, 9th Edition]

T1:

Tumor confined to the kidney, no more than 7 cm in greatest dimension.

  • T1a: Tumor 4 cm or less.
  • T1b: Tumor greater than 4 cm but no more than 7 cm.

T2:

Tumor confined to the kidney, greater than 7 cm in greatest dimension.

  • T2a: Tumor greater than 7 cm but no more than 10 cm.
  • T2b: Tumor greater than 10 cm.

T3:

Tumor invades perirenal or peripelvic adipose tissue, the pelvicalyceal system, or major veins, but remains confined within the renal fascia.

  • T3a: Tumor extends into the renal vein or its segmental branches; invades perirenal or peripelvic adipose tissue; or invades the pelvicalyceal system.
  • T3b: Tumor extends into the inferior vena cava below the diaphragm.
  • T3c: Tumor extends into the inferior vena cava above the diaphragm or invades the vena caval wall.

T4:

Tumor invades beyond the renal fascia, including contiguous extension into the ipsilateral adrenal gland.

N:

Lymph node involvement.

  • N0: No lymph node metastases.
  • N1: Metastasis in one or more regional lymph nodes.

M:

Distant metastases.

  • M0: No distant metastases.
  • M1: Distant metastases.

G:

WHO/ISUP grading for clear cell and papillary renal cell carcinomas.

  • G1: Nucleoli absent or inconspicuous at 400-fold magnification.
  • G2: Nucleoli clearly visible at 400-fold magnification.
  • G3: Nucleoli clearly visible at 100-fold magnification.
  • G4: Marked nuclear pleomorphism, tumor giant cells, or rhabdoid or sarcomatoid differentiation.

Stage Grouping According to UICC

  • Stage I: T1 N0 M0
  • Stage II: T2 N0 M0
  • Stage III: T3 N0 M0 or T1–T3 N1 M0
  • Stage IV: T4 N0–1 M0 or T1–4 N0–1 M1

Macroscopic Pathology

Classic renal cell carcinoma has a yellow-brown cut surface and a heterogeneous appearance due to hemorrhage and necrosis [fig. surgical specimen of renal cell carcinoma]. It is relatively well demarcated from the renal tissue. Infiltrative growth is more common in tumors with sarcomatoid or rhabdoid differentiation and in advanced tumors. Other patterns include cystic growth [fig. cystic clear cell renal cell carcinoma and fig. cystic renal cell carcinoma]. Calcifications are found in 10–20 %. Renal vein invasion occurs in 10 %; continuous tumor growth may form a tumor thrombus that can extend into the right atrium [fig. large tumor thrombus in the inferior vena cava].


CT showing a large tumor thrombus from a right-sided renal cell carcinoma (horizontal plane on the left and frontal plane on the right). The arrow marks the thrombus (left) and its cranial extension (right). With kind permission, Prof. Dr. K. Bohndorf, Augsburg.

Microscopic Pathology

The 2022 WHO classification of renal tumors considers morphological, molecular genetic, and prognostic differences.

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma:

70–80 %. The tumor has a yellow-brown cut surface. Abundant glycogen and fat in the cytoplasm give the cells a clear appearance on conventional staining [fig. histology of clear cell renal cell carcinoma]. The tumor originates from the proximal tubule and is highly vascularized. Eosinophilic, sarcomatoid, rhabdoid, and mixed differentiation patterns also occur. Alterations in the VHL signaling pathway are detectable in most tumors.

Histology of clear cell renal cell carcinoma: Abundant glycogen and fat in the cytoplasm give the cells a clear appearance on conventional staining. Image by Dr. Edwin P. Ewing, Jr., Public Health Image Library, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, USA, https://phil.cdc.gov/.

Papillary renal cell carcinoma:

10 %. Basophilic (type 1) or eosinophilic (type 2) cells form papillary or tubular patterns. The tumor originates from the proximal tubule and is poorly vascularized. Compared with clear cell renal cell carcinoma, the prognosis is better for localized disease but worse for metastatic disease (Steffens et al., 2012).

Chromophobe renal cell carcinoma:

5 %. This tumor originates from the cortical portion of the collecting duct. The name refers to the lack of staining of the cytoplasm in the large polygonal cells. Numerous microvesicles containing a mucopolysaccharide are visible by electron microscopy. Chromophobe renal cell carcinoma and oncocytoma are related oncocytic tumors. For localized disease, the prognosis is better than that of conventional renal cell carcinoma.

Collecting duct carcinoma or Bellini duct carcinoma:

Approximately 1 %. The prognosis is very poor. Histology shows infiltrative, tubular, or tubulopapillary growth in a dense, collagen-rich stroma, usually in the renal medulla.

Renal medullary carcinoma:

Less than 1 %. It occurs almost exclusively in patients with sickle cell disease and originates in the renal medulla. Its histology and prognosis resemble those of collecting duct carcinoma, but it is classified as a separate aggressive entity.

Mucinous tubular and spindle cell carcinoma:

Less than 1 %, occurring mainly in women. This tumor is usually indolent and contains tubular and spindle cell components in a mucinous stroma.

Multilocular cystic renal neoplasm with low malignant potential:

A cystic tumor with low-grade tumor cells. Metastases have not been reported in numerous studies; therefore, the former term multilocular cystic renal cell carcinoma is no longer used.

MiT family translocation renal cell carcinoma:

This tumor type results from overexpression of transcription factors in the MiT family and primarily affects children and adolescents. It is usually detected at an advanced stage.

Succinate dehydrogenase-deficient renal cell carcinoma:

These tumors are characterized by loss of SDHB expression, a marker of mitochondrial dysfunction. The solid tumors have a brown cut surface and cytoplasmic vacuoles. They primarily affect young adults, and many patients have a germline mutation. The prognosis is favorable.

Fumarate hydratase-deficient renal cell carcinoma:

Fumarate hydratase-deficient renal cell carcinoma is a rare, molecularly defined, and usually aggressive RCC entity caused by inactivation of the FH gene. It can occur sporadically or be inherited as part of HLRCC syndrome and may histologically resemble papillary renal cell carcinoma.

Other rare molecularly defined renal cell carcinomas:

ALK-rearranged renal cell carcinomas and ELOC-mutated renal cell carcinomas.

Unclassifiable renal cell carcinomas:

4–7 % of renal cell carcinomas, usually large and undifferentiated, cannot be classified definitively. The prognosis is often poor.

Oncocytoma:

5 %. See the section on oncocytoma.

Metastatic Pattern

In descending order of frequency: lung, lymph nodes, bone, liver, adrenal gland, brain, and skin.
