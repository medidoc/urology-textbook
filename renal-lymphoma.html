Diagnosis and Treatment of Renal Lymphoma

Definition

Renal lymphoma is usually a secondary renal manifestation of non-Hodgkin lymphoma; primary renal lymphoma is very rare (Sheth et al., 2006).

Etiology and Pathology of Renal Lymphoma

Origin and growth:

Renal involvement in systemic lymphoma is usually secondary, through hematogenous spread or direct extension. Multiple smaller lymphoma foci in the kidney may coalesce to form a radiologically visible mass.

Risk factors for renal lymphoma:

  • Known lymphoma
  • Iatrogenic immunosuppression
  • HIV infection
  • Autoimmune diseases
  • Prior radiotherapy or chemotherapy

Lugano Classification of Lymphomas

The Lugano classification is the current update of the Ann Arbor classification and is used for Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphomas.

Stage I:

Involvement of a single lymph node region or a single extranodal organ.

Stage II:

Involvement of two or more lymph node regions on the same side of the diaphragm, or localized extranodal involvement with regional lymph nodes.

Stage III:

Involvement of lymph node regions on both sides of the diaphragm.

Stage IV:

Disseminated involvement of one or more extralymphatic organs.

Modifiers:

The following letters are appended to the stage to provide further information about the disease:

  • A – absence of B symptoms
  • B – presence of B symptoms
  • E – localized extranodal involvement
  • S – splenic involvement
  • X – bulky disease, defined in adults as a tumor mass with a maximum diameter > 10 cm

Signs and Symptoms of Renal Lymphoma

  • Flank pain or hematuria
  • B symptoms: fever, weight loss, or night sweats
  • Renal insufficiency, particularly with bilateral diffuse infiltration, obstruction, or tumor lysis

Diagnostic Workup

Laboratory tests:

Differential blood count, creatinine, electrolytes, uric acid, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and urinalysis.

Bone marrow biopsy:

Performed for staging depending on the lymphoma stage and histology.

CT of the abdomen and thorax or PET/CT:

Lymphomas may show different growth patterns: a solitary lesion, multiple lesions, diffuse infiltration of the kidney with organ enlargement, perirenal soft-tissue involvement, or direct invasion from hilar or retroperitoneal lymph nodes. Lesions are typically hypovascular and show homogeneous or mildly heterogeneous contrast enhancement. Additional findings that suggest renal lymphoma include splenomegaly, marked retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, or lymphadenopathy in other regions.

Tumor biopsy:

Percutaneous core-needle biopsy with histologic and additional molecular pathologic evaluation is essential for diagnosis and for avoiding unnecessary nephrectomy.

Therapy of Renal Lymphoma

Treatment depends on the lymphoma subtype, stage, the patient's general condition, and organ function; see textbooks of internal medicine for a detailed discussion. Immunochemotherapy (e.g., R-CHOP) is commonly administered. Local radiotherapy may be considered for selected localized or residual lesions. The urologist's central role is to avoid unnecessary nephrectomy and manage urologic complications.

Prognosis

Secondary renal involvement usually reflects advanced systemic lymphoma and is associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Older series reported a 5-year survival rate of approximately 30 %; better outcomes have been reported with rituximab-containing therapy.
