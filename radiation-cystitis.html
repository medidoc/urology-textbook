Symptoms, Diagnosis, and Treatment of Radiation Cystitis

Definition

Radiation cystitis is damage to the urinary bladder after radiation therapy for malignancies of the bladder or adjacent organs (Nuhn and Thüroff, 2022).

Epidemiology

Lower urinary tract symptoms develop in 5–21 % of patients after radiation therapy to the pelvic region. Up to 9 % develop moderate or severe gross hematuria due to radiation cystitis.

Etiology

The severity of the disease depends on the radiation dose. Three phases of tissue response to irradiation are distinguished.

Acute reaction:

An inflammatory response with tissue edema and hyperemia develops within 4–6 weeks. Because the bladder epithelium regenerates relatively slowly, this phase occurs later than it does in the intestinal epithelium. After the epithelium regenerates, the radiation injury either heals or progresses to the second phase of the tissue response.

Ischemic reaction:

In the second phase, necrosis of the vascular endothelium and fibrosis of the perivascular connective tissue cause ischemic tissue damage. Histologically, the findings are those of obliterative endarteritis. Over time, the tissue becomes increasingly vulnerable to external influences, and its capacity to heal is markedly reduced. Recurrent episodes of gross hematuria occur clinically in response to inflammation. A bladder fistula may develop.

Fibrotic reaction:

As a third tissue response, progressive fibrosis and contraction develop as a consequence of ischemia. Fibrotic contraction may occur more than 10 years after radiation therapy.

Signs and Symptoms

  • Hematuria, dysuria, urinary frequency, nocturia, and recurrent urinary tract infections.
  • Urinary incontinence and pneumaturia are clinical signs of a bladder fistula.

Diagnosis

History:

Hematuria? Radiation dose, treatment fields, type of radiation?

Cystoscopy:

Cystoscopy is performed for differential diagnosis, with biopsy if necessary. Typical findings are an atrophic, pale mucosa with corkscrew-shaped vascular telangiectasias. The urothelium is vulnerable; sharply demarcated bleeding ulcers may be seen, particularly after substantial bladder filling.

Imaging:

Voiding cystourethrography if a fistula is suspected or to rule out vesicoureteral reflux before intravesical therapy.

Treatment

Acute cystitis after irradiation:

A common, accepted adverse effect, occurring in up to 60 % of patients receiving radiation therapy. Symptomatic treatment includes anticholinergics, NSAIDs, and/or bladder instillations to restore the GAG layer, for example, with chondroitin sulfate (Madersbacher et al., 2012). In patients with gross hematuria, diuresis may be increased; bladder irrigation may be necessary.

Endoscopic therapy (TURB):

Indicated for bladder tamponade, persistent gross hematuria, or unexplained gross hematuria requiring diagnostic confirmation. Transurethral resection and coagulation may be performed.

Oral therapy:

ε-aminocaproic acid is a fibrinolysis inhibitor (an antagonist of urokinase) and is excreted by the kidneys in active form in 80 % of patients. Dosage: up to 35 mg/kg body weight orally four times daily (1-1-1-1). Indication: persistent gross hematuria after endoscopic hemostasis and complete evacuation of clots (off-label treatment).

Intravesical therapy:

The following substances may be instilled for treatment-refractory gross hematuria (off-label treatment):

ε-aminocaproic acid:

200 mg/l in 0.9 % sodium chloride solution as continuous bladder irrigation until 24 h after the hematuria has stopped.

Alum:

Potassium aluminum sulfate as a 1 % solution. Irrigate the bladder at 100–300 ml/h. Aluminum toxicity may occur with treatment lasting several days, particularly in patients with impaired renal function.

Formalin:

Use a 1 % solution. Instill under anesthesia for 10 min. Hemostasis results from denaturation of the superficial tissue layers. Because instillation causes severe pain, it can be performed only under anesthesia.

The rate of adverse effects is high: ureteral strictures, bladder perforation, bladder fistulas, renal failure, contracted bladder, and anuria. Before treatment, a cystogram is required to rule out vesicoureteral reflux. Position the patient with the upper body elevated. Fill the bladder by gravity, using a 15 cm water column, until it reaches capacity. After 10 min, drain the formalin and rinse the bladder with distilled water.

Silver nitrate:

Use a 0.5–1 % solution. Application as described for formalin.

Additional treatment options:

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy:

Reduces ischemic injury and may improve hematuria and lower urinary tract symptoms over the long term. Treatment protocols vary considerably: 30–40 sessions, oxygen at 2–3 atm, and a treatment duration of 75–120 min. Symptoms improve in a large proportion of patients; complete remission of hematuria is reported in approximately half. Adverse effects are rare.

Angioembolization:

In patients with life-threatening bleeding that cannot be controlled endoscopically or by intravesical treatment, selective embolization of the vesical branches of the internal iliac artery may be considered.

Palliative cystectomy:

Indications include uncontrollable gross hematuria, bladder fistula, contracted bladder, and treatment-refractory pain. Morbidity and mortality are higher than for cystectomy without prior radiation therapy. Urinary diversion may be performed with a colonic conduit or cutaneous ureterostomy; the small intestine is often unsuitable because of prior radiation.
