Treatment and Prevention of Bladder infection (Bacterial Cystitis)

  • Bladder infection: Epidemiology, Symptoms and Diagnosis
  • Bladder infection: treatment and prevention of acute bladder infection

Treatment of Bladder Infection without Antibiotics

Management of asymptomatic bacteriuria

In the vast majority of patients without symptoms, screening (urine culture in the absence of symptoms) and prophylactic antibiotic therapy are not indicated .

Symptomatic Treatment of Mild Cystitis

In mild acute cystitis, watchful waiting with symptomatic treatment and no antibiotics is justified and is consistent with the S3 guideline. Because a urine culture is generally not available when treatment begins, choosing an antibiotic would require empirical therapy. Increased fluid intake, warm sitz baths, analgesics, and anticholinergics may be prescribed while awaiting culture results. If symptoms do not improve sufficiently with symptomatic treatment, antibiotic therapy is indicated (see below). The spontaneous cure rate of acute uncomplicated cystitis after one week is 30–50 %.

Antibiotic Therapy for Uncomplicated Cystitis

Table Antibiotic options provides an overview of current resistance rates for commonly used antibiotics. First-line antibiotics for uncomplicated cystitis are fosfomycin, nitrofurantoin, nitroxoline, pivmecillinam, trimethoprim, or cotrimoxazole . They provide adequate efficacy with a low risk of adverse effects, resistance selection, or disruption of the intestinal flora.

Fosfomycin:

A single dose of 3 g fosfomycin results in urinary excretion of the drug at therapeutic concentrations for 2–4  days. Fosfomycin is a first-line treatment for uncomplicated cystitis in women.

Nitrofurantoin:

Nitrofurantoin is excreted in the urine and is a first-line treatment for uncomplicated cystitis. Dosage: 100 mg sustained-release formulation 1-0-1 or 1-1-1 for 5–7  days. The maximum daily dose for children and adults should not exceed 5 mg/kg body weight.

Nitroxoline:

Nitroxoline is excreted in the urine and is a first-line treatment for uncomplicated cystitis. Dosage: 250 mg 1-1-1 for 5–10  days.

Penicillins:

Pivmecillinam 400 mg 1-1-1 or amoxicillin with clavulanic acid 875/125 mg 1-0-1 for 3  days. Pivmecillinam is a first-line treatment for uncomplicated cystitis. Amoxicillin with clavulanic acid carries a higher risk of adverse effects and is a second-line treatment.

Cephalosporins:

For example, cefpodoxime 100 mg 1-0-1 or cefuroxime 500 mg 1-0-1; cefdinir or cefaclor are alternatives. Treatment duration is 3  days. Cephalosporins can select for ESBL-producing bacteria or cause Clostridioides difficile infection. They are second-line agents, for example, when first-line agents are contraindicated or treatment has failed.

Trimethoprim or cotrimoxazole:

Trimethoprim 200 mg or cotrimoxazole 960 mg (trimethoprim 160 mg and sulfamethoxazole 800 mg), 1-0-1 for 3  days. These are first-line treatments because they are well tolerated, but resistance rates may be as high as 30 %.

Fluoroquinolones:

Ofloxacin 100–200 mg 1-0-1, ciprofloxacin 250–500 mg 1-0-1, or levofloxacin 250–500 mg 1-0-0 for 3  days. Caution: Because of their potentially serious adverse effects, fluoroquinolones are third-line agents and are approved only when no alternative treatment is available .

Treatment of Cystitis in Men or Complicated Cystitis in Women

After obtaining a urine culture, start treatment with an oral cephalosporin or an aminopenicillin with a β-lactamase inhibitor for 7  days. Adjust the antibiotic if necessary after the culture results are available.

Treatment of Cystitis during Pregnancy

Amoxicillin 250 mg 1-1-1 or an oral cephalosporin, such as cefpodoxime 100–200 mg 1-0-1, may be used. Asymptomatic bacteriuria should also be treated during pregnancy because otherwise the risk of acute pyelonephritis is approximately 30 % [see urinary tract infections in pregnancy].

Treatment of Cystitis in Children

Oral treatment for 2–4  days is often sufficient; other studies support treatment for 7–10  days. Suitable antibiotics for children include cotrimoxazole, oral cephalosporins, and amoxicillin.

Treatment of Candida Cystitis

Do not treat funguria in the absence of urinary tract infection symptoms. Serial antibody titers may provide further evidence of a clinically relevant infection. Treatment options include fluconazole 200 mg 1-0-0 for 14  days, bladder irrigation with amphotericin B, or a single intravenous dose of amphotericin B.

Prevention of Recurrent Cystitis

See also cranberry.

Cystitis after Sexual Intercourse

The following measures reduce the recurrence rate: avoiding genital contact, urinating after sex, maintaining high urine output, avoiding spermicides and diaphragms for contraception, and avoiding anal intercourse.

A single dose of a cephalosporin, cotrimoxazole, or nitrofurantoin after sexual intercourse reduces the frequency of cystitis, especially in women who use a diaphragm or spermicide.

Cranberry Juice

Cranberries are related to lingonberries. Regular consumption of cranberry juice (twice daily) may reduce the frequency of urinary tract infections. Proposed mechanisms include reduced bacterial adherence to the urothelium and bacteriostatic properties of cranberries. Overall, however, the evidence is very inconsistent, and the therapeutic effect in adults is very small in practice . Several randomized studies have demonstrated an effect of cranberry juice in children.

Vaginal Estrogen Therapy

In postmenopausal women with recurrent urinary tract infections, estrogen replacement therapy reduces the frequency of UTIs. Vaginal estrogen, administered as a capsule, cream, or estrogen-releasing ring, is more effective and has fewer adverse effects than oral hormone replacement therapy.

Oral Immunoprophylaxis

Oral immunoprophylaxis with inactivated but immunogenic E. coli strains (e.g., Uro-Vaxom) has a protective effect, reducing the recurrence rate by 22–65 % over 6–12  months. Dosage: one oral capsule daily for 3  months.

Parenteral Immunoprophylaxis

Intramuscular vaccination has also proved effective in controlled studies, reducing UTI recurrences and breakthrough infections by 26–93 % compared with placebo . For Strovac or Perison, the primary immunization consists of three intramuscular injections at 2-week intervals, followed by a booster after approximately one year.

D-mannose

Taking 2 g of D-mannose daily reduces the frequency of urinary tract infections . Excreted D-mannose binds to the fimbriae of coliform bacteria and reduces their adherence to the urothelium. A large randomized study found no protective effect of D-mannose for prevention .

Low-Dose Antibiotic Prophylaxis

This is considered an effective method for preventing recurrent urinary tract infections in patients with substantial symptom burden or complications. It is taken once daily in the evening or as a single dose after sexual intercourse. Possible agents and dosages are nitrofurantoin 50–100 mg, trimethoprim 50–100 mg, or cotrimoxazole 240–480 mg. Long-term use of fluoroquinolones such as ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, or norfloxacin is not approved. Concerns include adverse effects, increasing resistance, and recurrence rates that often remain unchanged after antibiotic prophylaxis is discontinued .

Prognosis

Acute uncomplicated cystitis has an excellent prognosis and usually resolves without sequelae with appropriate treatment. The spontaneous cure rate is also high without antibiotic treatment, although symptoms last longer. In the absence of complicating risk factors, the risk of an ascending infection is below 1 %.
