Wilms Tumor: Diagnosis and Treatment of Nephroblastoma

Definition of Wilms Tumor

Wilms tumor (also called nephroblastoma) is the most common malignant kidney tumor in children. It arises from remnants of immature tissue from kidney development (Kalapurakal et al., 2004) (Wu et al., 2005) (GPOH guideline). German surgeon Max Wilms (1867–1918) described the disease in 1899.

Epidemiology

The annual incidence is approximately 1 per 100,000 children younger than 15 years, corresponding to about 100 new cases per year in Germany. The median age at diagnosis is 3.5 years. The sex ratio is balanced. Ten percent of children with Wilms tumor have one of the genetic syndromes listed below.

Association with rare genetic syndromes

Denys-Drash syndrome:

Gonadal dysgenesis, early-onset nephrotic proteinuria due to diffuse mesangial sclerosis, an increased risk of Wilms tumor (up to approximately 50 %), and pathogenic WT1 variants (see below).

WAGR syndrome:

Wilms tumor (in 50 % of cases), Aniridia, Genital anomalies, and intellectual disability (Retardation); associated with WT1 mutations (see below).

Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome:

A 4–10 % risk of Wilms tumor; excessive growth at the cellular level, organ level (macroglossia, nephromegaly, hepatomegaly), or body-segment level (hemihyperplasia); and a WT2 alteration (see below).

Perlman syndrome:

A very rare genetic syndrome characterized by features including polyhydramnios, macrosomia, nephromegaly, and a high risk of Wilms tumors.

Fanconi anemia, complementation group D1:

Mutations in the BRCA2 gene confer a high risk of Wilms tumors and medulloblastomas.

Horseshoe kidney:

The risk of Wilms tumor is increased twofold to sevenfold.

Causes (Etiology) of Nephroblastoma

Wilms tumor is a classic example of tumor development following loss of tumor-suppressor gene function (the Knudson hypothesis). Because cells are diploid, two genetic events are required to completely inactivate a tumor-suppressor gene. In patients with genetic syndromes or familial Wilms tumor, the first mutation is inherited from a parent; a second mutation in a renal cell leads to tumor development later. However, most Wilms tumors arise sporadically.

Wilms tumor suppressor gene 1 (WT1):

WT1 is located on chromosome 11p13. Its gene product regulates the expression of other genes during embryonic kidney development.

WT2:

Located on chromosome 11p15, WT2 leads to overexpression of IGF2 through loss of imprinting.

Further molecular changes:

P53 mutations occur in tumors with anaplastic histology. Loss of chromosomal arms 16 and 1 is typical during tumor progression.

Wilms Tumor Pathology

Classic Wilms tumor:

Islands of metanephric blastema with variable epithelial and stromal components (a triphasic tumor).

Anaplastic Wilms tumor:

Marked nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia, and abnormal mitotic figures are characteristic. Approximately 10 % of Wilms tumors are anaplastic. These tumors have a poor prognosis in advanced stages because of their resistance to chemotherapy.

Wilms tumor with nephrogenic rests:

Persistent embryonic cells (nephrogenic rests, or nephroblastomatosis) are found in normal renal tissue in 30 % of kidneys with Wilms tumor. Nephrogenic rests may be perilobar or intralobar. Perilobar nephrogenic rests (PLNR) suggest a later developmental disturbance than intralobar nephrogenic rests (ILNR). Their presence carries a high risk of bilateral or metachronous disease.

Metastases:

Distant metastases are present at diagnosis in 10 % of cases. They occur most commonly in the lungs and less often in the liver or lymph nodes. Brain and bone metastases are extremely rare.

Tumor Staging

SIOP staging is performed after neoadjuvant chemotherapy and surgery:

Stage I:

The tumor is confined to the kidney and has been completely resected. The capsule is intact.

Stage II:

The tumor extends beyond the renal capsule into the perirenal fat, renal sinus, or lymphatic vessels, but has been completely resected.

Stage III:

Incomplete resection, lymph node involvement, tumor rupture, or a previous biopsy.

Stage IV:

Hematogenous distant metastases.

Stage V:

Bilateral nephroblastoma at initial diagnosis.

Signs and Symptoms

An abdominal mass, abdominal pain, and hematuria are typical findings. Renin secretion causes hypertension in 25 % of patients; vomiting, fever, and varicocele are less common. Lung metastases may cause cough or dyspnea. Evaluate patients for associated malformations, including aniridia, hemihyperplasia, and genital anomalies.

Diagnostic Workup

Laboratory tests:

No specific tumor markers are available. Useful tests include a complete blood count, renal function tests, electrolytes, liver tests, coagulation studies, and urinalysis. Analyze a 24-hour urine collection for catecholamines to rule out neuroblastoma.

Ultrasound:

Shows a renal mass. Doppler ultrasound may detect involvement of the renal veins or inferior vena cava.

CT or, preferably, MRI of the abdomen:

Imaging cannot reliably distinguish Wilms tumor from other solid tumors in children, such as neuroblastoma or renal lymphoma. Imaging also helps assess resectability, estimate renal function on the contralateral side, and detect bilateral disease.

Chest CT:

Used to detect metastases.

MIBG scintigraphy:

Consider when the diagnosis cannot be clearly distinguished from neuroblastoma.

Tumor biopsy:

Under the SIOP protocol, biopsy is not indicated in children of the typical age (six months to 16 years) with typical imaging findings, to avoid upstaging to stage III. Consider biopsy when imaging or age is atypical or a non-Wilms tumor is suspected.

Genetic testing and counseling:

Indicated in patients with risk factors, including bilateral or multicentric tumors, very young age at diagnosis, nephrogenic rests, associated syndromes, or a positive family history.

Treatment of Wilms Tumor

Because case numbers are small, treatment should follow the recommendations of large collaborative studies. Two treatment approaches are used:

NWTS/COG recommendations

The National Wilms Tumor Study and the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) in the United States favor primary surgery without neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Postoperative histology and tumor stage determine the need for adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Under NWTS/COG recommendations, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is particularly indicated for bilateral disease, a planned nephron-sparing approach, a tumor that cannot initially be resected with confidence, a risk of tumor rupture, or an extensive tumor thrombus in the inferior vena cava.

SIOP recommendations

The recommendations of the International Society of Pediatric Oncology (SIOP), used primarily in Europe, favor neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery. For children older than six months and younger than 16 years with typical imaging findings, primary tumor biopsy is not indicated. Neoadjuvant therapy reduces tumor stage before surgery and lowers the risk of incomplete resection or tumor rupture. After tumor nephrectomy, postoperative stage, histology, and molecular risk factors determine risk classification and guide adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Primary surgery is performed in patients younger than six months or older than 16 years because other malignant tumors are more likely in these age groups.

Tumor nephrectomy

Generally performed through a transperitoneal approach. The operation includes complete tumor resection whenever possible and accurate intraoperative staging of the lymph nodes, liver, peritoneum, and inferior vena cava.

Chemotherapy

Always administered as combination chemotherapy. The most effective drugs are actinomycin D and vincristine. Anthracyclines (doxorubicin) are added for higher-stage tumors.

Radiotherapy

The indication depends on histology and local stage after neoadjuvant chemotherapy and tumor nephrectomy. Indications include local stage III disease, tumors with high-grade malignancy from local stage II onward, and lung metastases that respond inadequately to chemotherapy.

Screening

Perform ultrasound screening every 3–4 months in children with a greater than 5 % risk of Wilms tumor, including children with WAGR syndrome, Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, or familial Wilms tumor.

Prognosis

Ninety percent of children with nephroblastoma can be cured. Prognosis depends on tumor stage, histologic subtype, response to treatment, and molecular risk factors. Unfavorable prognostic factors include metastases, diffuse anaplasia, high-grade malignancy, blastemal-type histology after preoperative chemotherapy, and certain genetic aberrations, such as 1q gain and combined loss of heterozygosity at 1p and 16q.
