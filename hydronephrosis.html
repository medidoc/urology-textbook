Hydronephrosis and Upper Urinary Tract Obstruction: Differential Diagnosis

Urinary tract obstruction (urinary stasis) is impaired urinary outflow in the urinary tract. The resulting increase in pressure can lead to hydronephrosis, hydroureter, pain, infection, urosepsis, and loss of renal function.

Obstructive uropathy:

Obstructive uropathy describes functional or structural obstruction to urinary outflow.

Hydronephrosis:

Hydronephrosis, from the Greek words for water and kidney, describes dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces and does not always indicate clinically significant obstruction.

Grading of Hydronephrosis

Grade I:

Dilatation of the renal pelvis without dilatation of the calyces.

Grade II:

Dilatation of the renal pelvis, calyceal necks, and calyces without signs of parenchymal atrophy.

Grade III:

Marked dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces. Signs of organ atrophy include flattened papillae and blunting of the calyceal fornices.

Grade IV:

Massive dilatation of the renal pelvicalyceal system with loss of distinction between the renal pelvis and calyces. Marked atrophy of the renal parenchyma.

Differential Diagnosis

Ureteral diseases (intrinsic causes):

  • Ureteral calculi
  • Clots associated with gross hematuria [fig.]
  • Congenital disorders: ureteropelvic junction obstruction, megaureter, ureterocele, retrocaval ureter, or retroiliac ureter
  • Ureteral strictures after stone impaction or stone treatment, infection, tuberculosis, schistosomiasis, radiation, or iatrogenic ureteral injury
  • Tumors: fibroepithelial polyp or ureteral urothelial carcinoma

Iatrogenic causes:

  • Ureteral stricture after instrumentation, anastomosis, or ureteral reimplantation
  • Intraoperative injuries: Urologic procedures that may injure the ureter include ureteroscopy, prostatectomy, transurethral resections, and pelvic lymphadenectomy. Common procedures in other specialties that may injure the ureter include hysterectomy, rectal or sigmoid surgery, and vascular surgery.
  • Radiation

Diseases of the vascular system:

  • Aortic aneurysm
  • Aneurysm of the iliac vessels
  • Retrocaval or retroiliac ureter
  • Ovarian vein thrombosis: a rare cause of usually right-sided hydronephrosis.

Gynecological diseases:

  • Pregnancy: compression of the ureter by the enlarged uterus, more often on the right than on the left
  • Endometriosis: cyclic flank pain, dysuria, and gross hematuria
  • Ectopic pregnancy
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess
  • Pelvic floor weakness with organ prolapse
  • Ovarian cysts, ovarian carcinoma, and uterine leiomyoma

Diseases of the retroperitoneum:

  • Retroperitoneal tumors: lymphoma, sarcoma, and primary retroperitoneal germ cell tumors
  • Lymph node or distant metastases from prostate, cervical, breast, colon, gastric, or bladder cancer
  • Retroperitoneal fibrosis (Ormond disease)
  • Pelvic lipomatosis: rare disease with fat accumulation in the pelvic retroperitoneum and cranial displacement of the bladder and ureters.

Infravesical and functional causes:

  • Urinary retention due to benign prostatic obstruction, prostate cancer, urethral stricture, or bladder neck obstruction
  • Neurogenic bladder dysfunction or detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia

Diagnosis

Classification of hydronephrosis in grades I–IV (Beetz and others, 2001): longitudinal plane (top row) and horizontal plane (bottom row).
Grade I: dilatation of the renal pelvis without dilatation of the calyces.
Grade II: dilatation of the renal pelvis, calyceal necks, and calyces without signs of parenchymal atrophy.
Grade III: marked dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces with signs of organ atrophy, including flattened papillae and blunting of the calyceal fornices.
Grade IV: massive dilatation of the renal pelvicalyceal system with loss of distinction between the renal pelvis and calyces and marked atrophy of the renal parenchyma.

  • Laboratory tests include urinalysis, serum creatinine, electrolytes, and urine culture. In patients with fever or signs of infection, obtain a complete blood count, C-reactive protein, and blood cultures.
  • Ultrasound imaging is used to grade hydronephrosis [fig. above], assess renal parenchymal thickness [fig. above], look for renal stones or masses, and exclude urinary retention.
  • For suspected urolithiasis, obtain a low-dose noncontrast CT of the abdomen. If extrinsic obstruction is suspected, obtain contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen or MRI with contrast medium.
  • Renal scintigraphy differentiates functionally significant obstruction from dilatation of the calyceal system without obstruction and determines split renal function.
  • Retrograde pyelography and ureteroscopy can confirm the diagnosis, provide definitive treatment, or decompress the urinary tract with a ureteral stent.
