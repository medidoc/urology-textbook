Upper Tract Urothelial Cancer: TNM Staging, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Malignant tumors of the upper urinary tract arise from the urothelium of the ureter, renal pelvis, or calyces and are closely related pathologically to bladder carcinoma. See the EAU guidelines on UTUC. Synonym: transitional cell carcinoma of the upper urinary tract.

Retrograde pyelography: ureteral stricture due to upper tract urothelial carcinoma. With kind permission, Prof. Dr. R. Harzmann, Augsburg.

Epidemiology

  • Incidence: 2 per 100,000. Peak incidence at 70–90 years of age; female-to-male ratio of 1:2 to 1:3. 5–10 % of all urothelial malignancies arise in the upper urinary tract. See also incidence.
  • 2–4 % of patients with bladder carcinoma develop a recurrence in the upper urinary tract. After treatment for UTUC, approximately 30 % of patients develop a bladder recurrence.

Etiology of Upper Tract Urothelial Carcinoma

Although bladder carcinoma and UTUC are biologically related because both arise from urothelium, their causes and risk factors differ substantially. See also the section on bladder carcinoma. Important risk factors for UTUC include tobacco smoking, although its role is less dominant than in bladder carcinoma, exposure to aristolochic acid, and Lynch syndrome (5–20 %). Patients with UTUC should be evaluated for Lynch syndrome, particularly if they are younger than 60 years at diagnosis, have a personal or family history of Lynch-associated tumors, or have abnormal tumor immunohistochemistry. Tumor tissue should be tested for mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR). Abnormal findings should be confirmed by germline testing through clinical genetics.

TNM Tumor Stages of UTUC [UICC 2025]

T: Local Tumor Stage

  • Ta: Noninvasive papillary carcinoma.
  • Tis: Flat high-grade lesion without invasion.
  • T1: Tumor invades the subepithelial connective tissue.
  • T2: Tumor invades the muscular layer.
  • T3: Tumor invades the kidney, peripelvic fat, or periureteral fat.
  • T4: Tumor invades adjacent organs or extends through the kidney into the perinephric fat.

N: Regional Lymph Node Involvement

  • N0: No regional lymph node metastasis.
  • N1: Single lymph node metastasis with a diameter of less than 2 cm.
  • N2: Single lymph node metastasis with a diameter greater than 2 cm, or multiple lymph node metastases.

M: Distant Metastasis

  • M0: No distant metastasis.
  • M1: Distant metastasis present.

G: Grading

  • Urothelial papilloma.
  • Papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential (PUNLMP).
  • Low-grade urothelial carcinoma, broadly corresponding to the former categories G1 and parts of G2.
  • High-grade urothelial carcinoma, broadly corresponding to parts of G2 and G3.

Pathology of Upper Tract Urothelial Carcinoma

Histology:

Urothelial carcinomas account for more than 90 % of tumors. Pure squamous cell carcinomas and adenocarcinomas are rare. Histologic variants or divergent differentiation are associated with a poor prognosis.

Growth pattern:

Despite their papillary morphology, UTUCs invade more often than comparable urothelial carcinomas of the bladder [fig. UTUC of the renal pelvis]. Approximately half of UTUCs are muscle-invasive or locally advanced at diagnosis. Multifocal dysplastic areas or carcinoma in situ in the ipsilateral urothelium are common in specimens after nephroureterectomy.

Metastasis:

Proximal tumors spread through the lymphatics to para-aortic, paracaval, and hilar lymph nodes. Tumors in the middle third of the ureter spread to the iliac, para-aortic, and paracaval lymph nodes, while distal tumors spread to the obturator and iliac lymph nodes. Hematogenous metastases occur mainly in the lungs, liver, bones, and adrenal glands.

Signs and Symptoms of Upper Tract Urothelial Cancer

  • Hematuria and worm-like clots.
  • Dysuria, flank pain, or renal colic.
  • Hydronephrosis, dysuria with synchronous bladder involvement, or symptoms of advanced cancer such as weight loss, reduced performance, night sweats, or bone pain.

Diagnosis of Upper Tract Urothelial Cancer

Review literature: Browne et al. (2005) and Patschan et al. (2008).

Laboratory tests:

Obtain a complete blood count, creatinine, electrolytes, coagulation tests, liver enzymes, and alkaline phosphatase. If infection is suspected, also obtain C-reactive protein and a urine culture. Urinalysis often shows hematuria but is nonspecific.

Urine cytology:

Microscopic examination of exfoliated urothelial cells in voided urine or an upper urinary tract irrigation sample can detect high-grade tumors and carcinoma in situ. Well-differentiated tumors exfoliate cells less often, and distinguishing them from inflammatory changes is difficult. For the technique, see urine cytology.

Ultrasound imaging:

Renal ultrasound may show hydronephrosis, a mass in the renal sinus [fig. ultrasound imaging of UTUC], or indirect signs of loss of renal function. Bladder ultrasound can assess for a synchronous bladder tumor; abdominal ultrasound may reveal liver metastases. Ultrasound does not replace CT urography.

Ultrasound imaging and retrograde pyelography of UTUC in the left upper calyx, pT1 high-grade.

CT urography:

Multiphasic CT urography is the preferred method for diagnosis and local staging [fig. CT of UTUC in the renal pelvis and CT of proximal ureteral UTUC]. Typical findings include filling defects, focal wall thickening, contrast defects, hydronephrosis, reduced excretion, and signs of invasion into the renal parenchyma, periureteral fat, or peripelvic fat.

MR urography:

MR urography is an alternative when CT urography is contraindicated, but it is less sensitive.

Retrograde pyelography:

This is an additional test when imaging findings are unclear or as part of ureteroscopy [fig. retrograde pyelography of UTUC]. If CT urography is inconclusive, collect an upper urinary tract irrigation sample for cytology before administering contrast medium. Findings suggestive of UTUC include ureteral strictures, filling defects, or absent contrast in the calyces.

Ureteroscopy:

Rigid and/or flexible ureteroscopy allows visualization and inspection of the affected upper urinary tract [fig. ureteroscopy of UTUC]. Preoperative histologic confirmation is not mandatory for a clearly high-risk tumor when radical nephroureterectomy is planned, because it would not change the treatment decision. For unclear findings or planned kidney-sparing treatment, selective cytology and targeted biopsy are important for diagnosis and risk stratification.

Flexible ureteroscopy: papillary upper tract urothelial carcinoma of a renal calyx.

Cystoscopy:

Cystoscopy is performed at the initial diagnosis of UTUC and during follow-up to rule out synchronous or metachronous urothelial carcinoma of the lower urinary tract.

Staging of UTUC:

CT urography is the preferred method for diagnosis and local staging. For high-risk UTUC, chest CT should also be performed to look for metastases. Routine chest CT is not mandatory before planned kidney-sparing treatment for clinically clear low-risk UTUC. Bone scintigraphy is indicated when bone metastases are suspected.

Intravenous urography:

Intravenous urography is now a second-line test because it does not replace CT urography or staging. Findings suspicious for a tumor include filling defects, hydronephrosis, or loss of function [fig. intravenous urogram of UTUC].

Intravenous urogram showing a left proximal ureteral carcinoma with a filling defect and abrupt termination of contrast (<=), and grade III hydronephrosis.

Risk Stratification of Non-Metastatic UTUC

The EAU guidelines distinguish between low-risk and high-risk UTUC to identify patients who may benefit from less aggressive kidney-sparing treatment.

Low-risk UTUC:

All of the following factors must apply: unifocal tumor, tumor size < 2 cm, low-grade pathology or cytology, and no evidence of invasion on CT urography.

High-risk UTUC:

At least one of the following factors is present: hydronephrosis, tumor size > 2 cm, high-grade pathology or cytology, local invasion on imaging, multifocal disease, or variant histology.

Treatment of Low-Risk Non-Metastatic UTUC

Kidney-sparing surgery:

Kidney-sparing procedures are first-line treatment for low-risk tumors. For higher-risk tumors, they may be used when preserving renal function is essential for the patient. The high recurrence rate of high-grade tumors is a concern [fig. recurrence of UTUC].

Recurrence of right-sided UTUC after distal ureteral resection and reconstruction with a psoas hitch. With kind permission, Dr. N. Dreger and Prof. Dr. S. Roth, Wuppertal.

Surgical therapy of distal UTUC:

Distal ureterectomy and ureteroneocystostomy with psoas hitch or Boari flap.

UTUC in the mid-third of the ureter:

Ureteral segment resection and ureteroureterostomy.

UTUC in the proximal ureter and renal pelvis:

Kidney-sparing surgery is technically challenging and is rarely performed. Options include pyelotomy and tumor resection, partial resection of the renal pelvis, or partial nephrectomy, for example upper-pole resection for tumors of the upper calyceal system.

Multifocal ureteral tumors:

Ureteral resection with ileal interposition is used rarely, in selected cases.

Endoscopic treatment options:

The upper urinary tract is accessed retrogradely or antegradely. The tumor is resected endoscopically or, preferably, ablated with a laser; electrocautery is another option (Chew et al., 2005; Ho et al., 2005). A second-look ureteroscopy within 6–8 weeks is advisable because residual or recurrent tumors are common.

Adjuvant instillation therapy of the upper urinary tract:

Evidence for adjuvant instillation therapy with BCG or mitomycin C via a nephrostomy or ureteral stent is inconsistent, and its effectiveness remains controversial.

Chemoablation with mitomycin gel:

Chemoablation with a mitomycin-containing thermogel (Jelmyto) may be considered in selected patients with low-grade UTUC. In the single-arm phase III OLYMPUS trial, 58 % achieved a complete response; ureteral stricture was a relevant adverse effect. Jelmyto is approved only in the United States and is not available in Germany or the European Union.

Postoperative instillation therapy of the bladder:

A single postoperative intravesical dose of mitomycin C reduces the risk of bladder recurrence from tumor cell seeding and is also appropriate after kidney-sparing procedures. Extravasation should be ruled out by cystography before instillation.

Treatment of High-Risk Non-Metastatic UTUC

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy:

Retrospective studies and phase II data support neoadjuvant cisplatin-based combination chemotherapy for locally advanced or clinically node-positive high-risk UTUC, particularly because renal function is more often adequate for cisplatin before nephroureterectomy. Prospective randomized trials specifically evaluating neoadjuvant chemotherapy for UTUC are not yet available. For indications, administration, and results, see the section on chemotherapy for bladder carcinoma.

Adjuvant chemotherapy:

The randomized POUT trial demonstrated a clear benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy after nephroureterectomy in patients with pT2–4 and/or pN+: three-year disease-free survival was 71 % versus 46 %. Patients with adequate renal function received cisplatin and gemcitabine; patients with impaired renal function received carboplatin.

Adjuvant immunotherapy:

Nivolumab is approved as adjuvant treatment after radical resection of high-risk urothelial carcinoma: in the United States without a PD-L1 restriction, and in the European Union, including Germany, for tumors with PD-L1 expression in tumor cells of ≥ 1 %. However, the evidence for UTUC is less robust than for bladder carcinoma.

Radical nephroureterectomy:

Radical nephroureterectomy, including removal of the distal ureter and a bladder cuff, is the standard treatment for high-risk UTUC. Kidney-sparing techniques should be considered in selected patients with impaired renal function. Complete removal of the distal ureter and bladder cuff is oncologically important because leaving a ureteral stump carries a relevant risk of recurrence. See the steps of radical nephroureterectomy for details.

Surgical approaches:

Radical nephroureterectomy can be performed by open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted approaches. Minimally invasive procedures offer perioperative advantages but require oncologically sound technique, including complete removal of the distal ureter and bladder cuff and avoidance of tumor violation and urine extravasation. Worse oncologic outcomes have been reported after laparoscopic nephroureterectomy, particularly in locally advanced disease and when bladder cuff resection was inadequate. The indication for a minimally invasive approach should therefore be carefully considered in patients with high-risk UTUC, large tumor burden, suspected lymph node involvement, or technically uncertain distal ureteral resection.

Lymphadenectomy for UTUC:

Template-based lymphadenectomy should be considered for high-risk UTUC, particularly in muscle-invasive disease or when lymph node involvement is suspected clinically. The lymphatic drainage template depends on tumor location: para-aortic or interaortocaval/paracaval for renal pelvis and proximal ureteral tumors, and pelvic for distal ureteral tumors. Retrospective analyses suggest that complete lymphadenectomy probably improves local control and may improve prognosis.

Postoperative instillation therapy of the bladder:

A single postoperative bladder instillation with mitomycin C after nephroureterectomy reduces the risk of bladder recurrence (17 % versus 27 %).

Chemotherapy and immunotherapy for metastatic UTUC:

For indications, administration, and results, see the section on chemotherapy of metastatic bladder carcinoma. Enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab is an important first-line option for advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma. Platinum-based chemotherapy, potentially followed by maintenance therapy, and other subsequent-line treatments are alternatives.

Follow-Up of Upper Tract Urothelial Cancer

Follow-up should be adapted to the patient’s risk and depend on treatment, tumor grade and stage, history of bladder carcinoma, and type of kidney-sparing procedure. The aims are to detect metachronous bladder carcinoma, ipsilateral or contralateral recurrence, local recurrence, and distant metastases. The EAU guidelines distinguish between low-risk and high-risk UTUC.

Follow-up after nephroureterectomy for low-risk UTUC:

Perform cystoscopy at three, six, and 12 months, then annually. Perform CT urography annually or individualize imaging intervals based on ultrasound findings.

Follow-up after nephroureterectomy for high-risk UTUC:

Perform cystoscopy and urine cytology every three months for the first two years, then every six months, and annually from year six onward. Perform CT urography and chest CT every six months for the first two years, then annually.

Follow-up after kidney-sparing management:

After endoscopic treatment, a second-look ureteroscopy should be performed after 6–8 weeks. For low-risk UTUC, perform cystoscopy and CT urography at three months and six months, then annually for five years. Add ureteroscopy according to risk and findings. After kidney-sparing treatment of high-risk UTUC, cystoscopy, cytology, CT urography, chest CT, and ureteroscopy should be individualized according to risk.

Prognosis of Upper Tract Urothelial Carcinoma

5-year survival by tumor stage:

Prognosis depends mainly on tumor stage, lymph node status, grade, histologic variants, and resection status. In recent series, the tumor-specific 5-year survival rate is approximately 86 % for T1N0, 77 % for T2N0, 63 % for T3N0, and 39 % for T4N0 or node-positive disease. The prognosis is substantially worse in patients with distant metastases.
