Acute Kidney Injury: Causes and Symptoms

  • Acute kidney injury – acute renal failure: causes, pathophysiology, and symptoms
  • Acute kidney injury – acute renal failure: Diagnosis and treatment

Definition of Acute Kidney Injury

Acute kidney injury (AKI), also called acute renal injury, is a sudden decline in kidney function characterized by an increase in nitrogenous waste products, disturbances of water, electrolyte, and acid-base balance, and possible oliguria or anuria. The term acute kidney injury is more precise than acute renal failure because it includes even mild increases in serum creatinine. AKI is classified according to KDIGO criteria into three stages based on serum creatinine and urine output. It is defined by an increase in serum creatinine of at least 0.3 mg/dl within 48 hours, an increase to at least 1.5 times the known or presumed baseline within 7 days, or urine output below 0.5 ml/kg body weight/h for at least 6 hours.

Urine Output

Urine output is an important, but insufficient, measure of kidney function when considered alone. AKI may be oliguric, anuric, normuric, or polyuric. Nonoliguric AKI is particularly common with toxic or medication-induced tubular injury.

  • Polyuria: urine output greater than 3000 ml in 24 h or > 40–50 ml/kg body weight/24 h
  • Oliguria: urine output below 500 ml in 24 h or < 0.5 ml/kg body weight/h
  • Anuria: urine output below 100 ml in 24 h or < 50 ml/m2/h

Epidemiology of Acute Kidney Injury

Community-Acquired AKI

AKI is common, with an incidence of 2 per 1000 people per year in Europe; many cases are not diagnosed. Risk factors include older age, chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, cirrhosis, sepsis, volume depletion, nephrotoxic medications, and urinary obstruction.

Hospital-Acquired AKI

Acute kidney injury occurs in 10–20% of hospitalized patients and in 35–60% of patients in intensive care units (Alscher et al., 2019).

Etiology of Acute Kidney Injury

The classic classification distinguishes prerenal, intrinsic renal, and postrenal causes; mixed causes are common.

Prerenal AKI

Worsening of kidney function due to reduced renal perfusion (55% of all AKI). There is initially no intrinsic kidney disease, but prolonged ischemia can cause acute tubular injury.

Intravascular Volume Depletion

Trauma, burns, bleeding, anaphylactic shock, sepsis, pancreatitis, and dehydration.

Decreased Cardiac Output

Myocardial infarction, other cardiac diseases (cardiorenal syndrome), pulmonary embolism, and mechanical ventilation.

Reduced Renal Perfusion

ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs, anesthesia, hepatorenal syndrome, and hyperviscosity syndrome in multiple myeloma or polycythemia.

Intrinsic Renal AKI

Worsening of kidney function due to renal disease (45% of all AKI).

Large Renal Vessel Diseases

  • Renal artery stenosis
  • Renal artery aneurysm
  • Arteriovenous fistula
  • Aortic aneurysm with dissection
  • Vasculitis
  • Renal vein thrombosis
  • Renal artery embolism

Microvascular Diseases

  • Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis
  • Goodpasture syndrome
  • Postinfectious glomerulonephritis
  • Polyarteritis nodosa
  • Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (formerly Wegener granulomatosis)
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus
  • IgA vasculitis (formerly Henoch-Schönlein purpura)
  • Essential cryoglobulinemia
  • Thrombotic microangiopathies, including hemolytic uremic syndrome, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

Tubulointerstitial Diseases

  • Allergic interstitial nephritis caused by medications (antibiotics, NSAIDs, thiazides, ACE inhibitors, allopurinol, proton pump inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and other antineoplastic agents)
  • Infections such as severe bacterial pyelonephritis or viral diseases such as CMV
  • Leukemic infiltration
  • Idiopathic disease

Acute Tubular Injury

Ischemia, sepsis, shock, major surgery, rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis, and toxic substances (contrast agents, aminoglycosides, cisplatin, and others) can cause acute tubular injury. Tubular obstruction by sloughed cells can further impair kidney function. Kidney function may recover after tubular cell regeneration.

Postrenal AKI

Worsening of kidney function due to impaired urinary drainage (5% of all AKI). Severe postrenal AKI is usually caused by bilateral obstruction, obstruction of a solitary functioning kidney, or bladder outlet obstruction. However, unilateral upper urinary tract obstruction can also meet the KDIGO criteria for AKI.

Upper Urinary Tract Obstruction

  • Malignant obstruction
  • Retroperitoneal fibrosis (Ormond disease)
  • Ureteral stones
  • Bleeding
  • Ureteral injury

Bladder Outlet Obstruction and Urinary Retention

  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)
  • Prostate cancer
  • Neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction
  • Medications (anticholinergics and opioids)
  • Urethral strictures
  • Urethral valves
  • Phimosis

Pathophysiology of Acute Kidney Injury

Excess Extracellular Fluid Volume

Reduced sodium and water excretion leads to weight gain, arterial hypertension, dyspnea, and pulmonary edema.

Hyperkalemia

Potassium may rise rapidly in oliguria or anuria. Additional cell breakdown (tumor lysis, hemolysis, or rhabdomyolysis) or use of potassium-sparing medications can cause particularly severe hyperkalemia.

Metabolic Acidosis

Reduced proton excretion and impaired tubular bicarbonate regeneration cause metabolic acidosis. Sepsis, lactic acidosis, diabetic ketoacidosis, liver disease, shock, and tissue ischemia may worsen the acidosis.

Uremic Complications

Complications include pericarditis, encephalopathy, nausea, vomiting, pruritus, bleeding due to platelet dysfunction, and immune dysfunction.

Signs and Symptoms of Acute Kidney Injury

AKI causes no symptoms or only nonspecific symptoms. The underlying diseases are usually responsible for the clinical presentation.

Symptoms of Prerenal AKI

  • Thirst, oliguria, anuria, hypotension, tachycardia, and decreased skin turgor
  • Symptoms of the underlying conditions

Symptoms of Intrinsic Renal AKI

Findings suggestive of ischemia or nephrotoxicity; fever, rash, arthralgia, eosinophilia, nephritic urine sediment, proteinuria, hematuria, hypertension, edema, or extrarenal manifestations of vasculitis or systemic disease.

Symptoms of Postrenal AKI

Flank or lower abdominal pain, urinary retention, incontinence, or neurologic symptoms may suggest postrenal AKI.

Symptoms of Complications

  • Dyspnea, muscle weakness, and paresthesia
  • Arrhythmias
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Delirium or coma
  • Bleeding tendency
  • Hypotension and shock
