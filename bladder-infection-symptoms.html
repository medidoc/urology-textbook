Etiology and Symtoms of Bladder Infection (bacterial cystitis)

  • Bladder infection: Epidemiology, Symptoms and Diagnosis
  • Bladder infection: treatment and prevention of acute bladder infection

Definition

Acute cystitis is a bacterial infection of the urinary bladder and the most common manifestation of a urinary tract infection.

Uncomplicated urinary tract infection

According to the German S3 guideline, a urinary tract infection is uncomplicated if there are no relevant functional or anatomical abnormalities of the urinary tract, renal dysfunction, or comorbidities that promote infection. The EAU guideline strictly limits uncomplicated infection to premenopausal, nonpregnant women, whereas the S3 guideline explicitly includes postmenopausal women and women with well-controlled diabetes mellitus without microvascular complications.

Complicated urinary tract infection

All urinary tract infections that cannot be classified as uncomplicated. By definition, this includes all UTIs in men and pregnant women, as well as infections in the presence of anatomical abnormalities, bladder catheters, advanced renal disease, or immunosuppressive comorbidities.

Epidemiology of Bladder Infection

Prevalence of dysuria

20–30 % of adult women have an episode of dysuria once or more than once a year; half of these episodes are due to acute cystitis.

Prevalence of asymptomatic bacteriuria

Prevalence is below 1 % in young men and 5 % in healthy premenopausal women; it is 4–19 % in older healthy patients, 15–50 % in nursing home residents, and up to 90 % in patients with paraplegia .

Etiology of Bladder Infection

For pathogenesis, see urinary tract infection. The most common pathogens causing uncomplicated urinary tract infections are E. coli (80 %), followed by Proteus mirabilis, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, and Klebsiella pneumoniae [Table pathogen spectrum].

Pathology of Bacterial Cystitis

In the early stages of acute cystitis, the bladder wall shows hyperemia, edema, and infiltration by neutrophils. As the disease progresses, the mucosa is replaced by easily injured granulation tissue; shallow ulcers partly filled with exudate may develop. The lamina muscularis is usually not involved in the inflammation. In the late stages, without treatment, hemorrhage and necrosis may occur.

Signs and Symptoms

  • Irritative bladder symptoms: urinary frequency, intense urgency that may progress to urge incontinence, dysuria, and burning after urination.
  • Suprapubic pain.
  • Cloudy urine or hematuria.
  • Complications:
    • Ascending infection with pyelonephritis; risk factors include vesicoureteral reflux and pregnancy.
    • Emphysematous cystitis [Fig. emphysematous cystitis on excretory urography].

Diagnosis of Bladder Infection

Diagnostic Workup of Uncomplicated Cystitis in Healthy Women

In healthy women with typical symptoms of acute uncomplicated cystitis (dysuria and urinary frequency without vaginal discharge), many of the diagnostic tests listed below can be omitted. Urinalysis, urine culture, and imaging are not essential because the probability of acute cystitis exceeds 70 %, and further testing does not improve diagnostic accuracy when the clinical presentation is clear. Antibiotic therapy may be initiated on the basis of symptoms alone .

Diagnostic Workup of Urinary Tract Infections

Consider the following tests in patients with fever, all men, and women with clinical signs of a complicated urinary tract infection.

Urinalysis:

Urinalysis by dipstick or microscopic examination may show pyuria, bacteriuria, microscopic or macroscopic hematuria, or a positive nitrite test when the bacterial count is high.

Urine culture:

Urine culture is recommended for complicated cystitis, in men and pregnant women, after unsuccessful treatment, and in patients with diabetes mellitus. At least 105 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml in a properly collected midstream urine sample indicate a UTI . Counts of 103–104 CFU/ml may already be clinically significant when symptoms are typical and a typical uropathogen is isolated in pure culture, without mixed bacterial growth. Forced diuresis or urinary frequency shortens the time urine remains in the bladder and may therefore result in falsely low bacterial counts despite cystitis. Bacterial growth in a urine sample obtained by single catheterization or bladder puncture is always pathological.

Ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder:

Renal ultrasound and bladder ultrasound assess for hydronephrosis, anatomical variants, postvoid residual urine, bladder diverticula, and bladder stones.

Pelvic examination:

Perform a pelvic examination if vaginitis or adnexitis is suspected; if confirmed, obtain swabs for microbiological testing.

CT or MR urography:

CT or MR urography is indicated for recurrent cystitis when ultrasound shows abnormalities requiring further evaluation. It is used to reliably exclude infection stones, obstruction, and complex anatomical abnormalities of the upper urinary tract. Conventional excretory urography is obsolete in routine diagnostic workup.

Cystoscopy:

Cystoscopy should be considered for recurrent cystitis or for the differential diagnosis of macroscopic hematuria after acute cystitis has resolved.

Voiding cystourethrography:

Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) should be considered in recurrent urinary tract infections when vesicoureteral reflux is suspected.

Differential Diagnosis of Bladder Infection

  • Vulvovaginitis.
  • Genital herpes or gonorrhea.
  • Other urinary tract infections: acute urethral syndrome, acute pyelonephritis, acute prostatitis, or acute urethritis.
  • Radiation cystitis.
  • Trauma: urethral irritation after sexual intercourse or instrumentation, or a foreign body in the bladder.
  • Cyclophosphamide chemotherapy.
  • Ketamine cystitis.
  • Interstitial cystitis.
  • Neurogenic bladder disorders.
  • Bladder cancer.
  • Psychosomatic symptoms.
