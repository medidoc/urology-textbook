Megaureter: Symptoms, Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition of Megaureter

A megaureter is a dilated ureter measuring more than 7–8 mm (Shokeir and Nijman, 2000) (Wilcox and Mouriquand, 1998) (EAU Guidelines). Megaureters are classified according to their cause:

Primary obstructive megaureter (POM):

Primary obstructive megaureter is a congenital ureteral dilatation caused by an aperistaltic ureteral segment at the ureterovesical junction. Abnormal embryologic development of the distal ureter leads to misorientation of the muscle fibers and impaired ureteral peristalsis [Fig. 1].

 Bilateral primary obstructive megaureters on excretory urography. With kind permission, Prof. Dr. R. Harzmann, Augsburg.

Secondary obstructive megaureter:

Increased intravesical pressure, increased bladder wall tension, or scarring causes obstruction where the ureter passes through the bladder wall, at the ureterovesical junction.

Refluxing megaureter:

Primary or secondary vesicoureteral reflux causes ureteral dilatation [Fig. 2].

Idiopathic nonobstructive, nonrefluxing megaureter:

Many megaureters in newborns fall into this category, although the cause often remains unclear. Increased urine production, delayed ureteral maturation, and subclinical obstruction have been discussed as contributing factors.

Obstructive and refluxing megaureter:

This rare form combines distal ureteral narrowing and vesicoureteral reflux.

Epidemiology of Megaureter

Megaureters account for approximately 20 % of cases of prenatally diagnosed hydronephrosis. Primary megaureters are more common in boys, are usually unilateral, and occur more often on the left.

Clinical Presentation of Megaureter

Megaureter is often diagnosed prenatally or incidentally on ultrasonography. Symptomatic children may present with febrile urinary tract infections, flank pain, hematuria, failure to thrive, or hydronephrosis with loss of function in the affected kidney.

Diagnosis of Megaureter

Ultrasonography:

Ultrasonography is an important initial test for distinguishing megaureter from ureteropelvic junction obstruction [Fig. 3]. The examination assesses the renal pelvis, calyces, parenchymal thickness, ureteral dilatation, distal ureter, bladder, and postvoid residual urine. A retrovesical ureteral diameter of 7 mm or greater is abnormal.

 Megaureter of the upper pole system in a right duplex kidney: the kidney shows dilatation of the upper pole calyces (A), and the retrovesical ureter is markedly dilated. Longitudinal (B) and transverse (C) views of the bladder. With kind permission, Dr. M. Fretschner, Augsburg.

MR urography:

MR urography is indicated for unexplained hydronephrosis, a duplex collecting system, suspected ectopic ureter, or discordant ultrasonography and scintigraphy findings. It provides morphologic and functional information to assess the condition.

Voiding cystourethrography:

Voiding cystourethrography confirms or excludes vesicoureteral reflux and bladder emptying disorders caused by posterior urethral valves.

Renal scintigraphy:

MAG3 scintigraphy determines split renal function and evaluates drainage. Findings that indicate significant obstruction include declining split renal function, delayed parenchymal transit, a curve that continues to rise or plateaus after furosemide, a half-time greater than 20 minutes, and significant residual activity after voiding. A half-time of less than 10 minutes argues against significant obstruction.

Excretory urography:

Excretory urography has largely been replaced by MR urography.

Retrograde pyelography:

Retrograde pyelography shows the anatomy and helps establish the differential diagnosis when imaging findings are unclear [Fig. 4], particularly when diagnosis is delayed until adulthood or when endoscopic intervention is planned.

 Primary obstructive megaureter on retrograde pyelography. With kind permission, Dr. R. Gumpinger, Kempten.

Whitaker test:

The renal pelvic pressure is measured through a nephrostomy during an infusion at 5–10 ml/min. The test is indicated when renal scintigraphy is inconclusive, particularly in patients with poor renal function.

Treatment of Megaureter

Primary obstructive megaureter:

Primary obstructive megaureter in children often resolves spontaneously. Indications for intervention (balloon dilatation or surgery) include significant obstruction on scintigraphy, recurrent febrile urinary tract infections, or progressive loss of renal function. Antibiotic prophylaxis during the first year of life is recommended for infants with megaureter, particularly when the distal ureter measures approximately 7 mm or more, to reduce the risk of febrile urinary tract infections.

Balloon dilatation:

Endoscopic balloon dilatation of the ureterovesical junction with temporary double-J ureteral stenting may be a less invasive alternative to open reconstruction in selected children. Reported success rates are heterogeneous; long-term cure rates of 25–90 % have been described (Kassite et al., 2018).

 Ureteral preparation before ureterocystostomy: the stenotic prevesical segment of the megaureter is excised. The ureter is stented with an 8-Fr double-J stent secured with rapidly absorbable suture. Plication of the ureteral wall (Starr technique) is suitable for moderately dilated ureters. Plication has fewer complications and does not carry a risk of ureteral wall ischemia or urine leakage.

Ureteral preparation before ureterocystostomy with excisional tapering: the stenotic prevesical segment of the megaureter is excised. The ureter is stented with an 8-Fr double-J stent secured with rapidly absorbable suture. In a severely dilated ureter, the lumen is reduced by resecting the excess ureteral wall and closing the ureter longitudinally. Care must be taken to preserve the ureteral blood supply.

Surgical treatment:

Surgical treatment includes excision of the narrow prevesical segment, ureteral remodeling when indicated (plication or vessel-sparing longitudinal partial resection; see figures above), and ureteroneocystostomy.

Secondary obstructive megaureter:

Treatment of the underlying disease is usually sufficient. Persistent dilatation, infections, or loss of renal function requires reassessment.

Refluxing megaureter:

See the section on vesicoureteral reflux. Conservative treatment is often sufficient. Surgical options include ureteroneocystostomy, with ureteral remodeling if indicated.

Idiopathic nonobstructive or nonrefluxing megaureter:

Observation with regular follow-up is sufficient when dilatation is stable or decreasing, febrile urinary tract infections are absent, and split renal function remains stable.
