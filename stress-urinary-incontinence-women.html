Stress Urinary Incontinence in Women: Epidemiology and Etiology

  • Stress urinary incontinence in women: epidemiology and etiology
  • Stress urinary incontinence: symptoms and diagnostic workup
  • Stress urinary incontinence: medical and surgical treatment

Definition

Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is involuntary urine leakage during physical exertion when bladder pressure exceeds urethral closure pressure as abdominal pressure rises.

Epidemiology

Vaginal deliveries

The prevalence of urinary incontinence is up to 10 % in nulliparous women, 16 % after cesarean section, and up to 21 % after vaginal delivery. In comparative studies, the prevalence of pure SUI was about 7 % after cesarean section versus 12 % after vaginal delivery.

Other risk factors

Age [see the following table], pelvic surgery, obesity, COPD, and postmenopausal estrogen deficiency.

Etiology and Pathogenesis

Components of the bladder closure mechanism

Bladder closure depends on urethral tone and active and passive pressure transmission during physical exertion.

Urethral tone

Urethral closure pressure at rest is generated by a sphincter system consisting of the rhabdosphincter (striated muscle) and lissosphincter (smooth muscle) [see section bladder anatomy].

Passive pressure transmission

During physical exertion, the rise in abdominal pressure is transmitted to the bladder neck and urethra. This requires intact connective tissue support of the urethra, bladder, and vagina.

Active pressure transmission

Active pressure transmission results from contraction of the striated pelvic floor and sphincter muscles during or immediately before physical exertion.

Causes of SUI

The main causes are urethral hypermobility and intrinsic sphincter deficiency (hypotonic urethra). Either factor, alone or in combination, can cause SUI. Neurologic disorders can also cause SUI; see neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction.

Hypermobile urethra

Caudal displacement of the urethra into the extra-abdominal compartment as abdominal pressure rises reduces pressure transmission, promotes opening of the bladder neck, and causes incontinence.

The stability of the suburethral connective tissue is a key factor in continence. Vaginal deliveries increase the risk of SUI through connective tissue damage, muscle tears, and injury to the innervation of the pelvic floor and sphincter. Pelvic organ prolapse may develop as a result.

Pelvic organ prolapse can cause or mask SUI. Masking is attributed to urethral kinking, known as the pinchcock effect. Severe urethral kinking can cause postvoid residual urine or urinary retention. After prolapse repair, previously masked incontinence may become clinically apparent. The effect of hysterectomy on SUI remains controversial.

Intrinsic sphincter deficiency

Intrinsic sphincter deficiency is insufficiency of the urethral sphincter, regardless of the anatomic position of the urethra.

  • Neurogenic causes include, in particular, sacral and peripheral lesions [see also neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction].
  • Scarring of the proximal urethra and bladder neck (e.g., after incontinence surgery or urethrotomy).
  • Aging and estrogen deficiency cause mucosal atrophy and can impair sphincter closure [see urethral mucosa].

Chronic increase in abdominal pressure

Chronic elevation of abdominal pressure can worsen existing SUI, e.g., with chronic bronchitis, obesity, hernias, or regularly carrying heavy loads.

Integral theory by Petros and Ulmsten

According to the integral theory, the anterior vaginal wall acts like a trampoline beneath the urethra and bladder and thereby contributes to continence. It is suspended in three dimensions within the pelvis. Depending on the location of the defect, three levels can be distinguished [see the following table], each associated with different clinical findings and therapeutic implications.
