Stress Urinary Incontinence in Women

  • Stress urinary incontinence in women: definition and etiology
  • Stress urinary incontinence: symptoms and diagnostic workup
  • Stress urinary incontinence: medical and surgical treatment

Signs and Symptoms

Stamey classification

Severity is classified as follows:

  • Grade 1: Urine loss with coughing, sneezing, or laughing.
  • Grade 2: Urine loss while walking or standing up.
  • Grade 3: Urine loss while lying down.

Other symptoms

Pelvic floor insufficiency with organ prolapse, urgency symptoms, postvoid residual urine, and recurrent urinary tract infections.

Diagnostic Evaluation

History

Ask about voiding symptoms, severity of incontinence (number of pads used), degree of bother, previous surgery, number of vaginal deliveries, medications (alpha blockers, clonidine), and neurologic and urologic disorders. Also ask about symptoms related to pelvic organ prolapse, defecation, and sexual function.

Urine testing

Urine sediment, dipstick urinalysis, and urine culture if infection is suspected. See also urine analysis and urinary tract infection.

Quantification of SUI

A voiding diary documenting fluid intake, voided volumes, and incontinence episodes is used to quantify SUI. A period of 24–48 h is usually sufficient. In cooperative patients with a digital scale, incontinence severity can be assessed by weighing the pads.

Pad test

An alternative to weighing pads as part of the voiding diary is to weigh a pad before and after use. The patient should drink plenty of fluids before the test. The pad is worn for one hour while the patient performs defined provocative exercises, such as climbing stairs, jumping, and coughing. Urine loss greater than 25 g is considered severe urinary incontinence.

Physical examination

In addition to an orienting neurologic examination, perform vaginal and rectal examinations with the patient in the lithotomy position.

Important abnormal findings include cystocele or rectocele, pelvic organ prolapse, reduced anal sphincter tone, absent anal reflex, absent clitoral or bulbocavernosus reflex, and urine leakage with straining, coughing, or the heel impact test.

Q-tip test

With the bladder full, a lubricated Q-tip is passed through the urethra to the bladder neck. Its mobility is observed during coughing. Rotation > 30 degrees is abnormal and suggests urethral hypermobility.

Marshall-Bonney test

The paraurethral tissue is elevated with the index and middle fingers while the patient coughs with a full bladder. The test can predict the effect of a suspension procedure.

Ultrasound imaging

Ultrasound is used to assess postvoid residual urine and bladder anatomy, including wall thickness and diverticula.

Introital ultrasound

This additional examination assesses bladder neck and urethral mobility, bladder neck opening, and associated pelvic organ prolapse. It is performed at rest with a full bladder and during straining. It is not essential in uncomplicated, unequivocal SUI.

Voiding cystourethrography and urodynamics

In patients with clinically clear, uncomplicated SUI and no other symptoms, urodynamics and voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) can be omitted before primary treatment. Urodynamic testing with VCUG should be performed for mixed urinary incontinence, reduced bladder capacity, voiding dysfunction, severe pelvic floor insufficiency, a coexisting neurologic disorder, or before repeat surgery [figure: voiding cystourethrography for SUI].

The primary purpose of urodynamics is less to demonstrate SUI than to exclude concomitant disorders that could compromise surgical outcomes. SUI can be demonstrated by urethral pressure profiling (resting profile, passive and active pressure transmission) and Valsalva leak point pressure (VLPP) measurement. Urine leakage may not be directly demonstrable with the measurement catheter in place, and direct demonstration is not essential. The Valsalva maneuver can also be performed without a urethral pressure catheter, using a rectal pressure probe.

Valsalva leak point pressure (VLPP)

With intra-abdominal and intravesical pressures measured, the patient performs a Valsalva maneuver with a full bladder until urine leakage occurs.

Cystoscopy

Cystoscopy is used in the differential diagnosis of incontinence, urinary frequency, or urgency symptoms. There are no specific cystoscopic signs of SUI.

Defecography or dynamic pelvic floor MRI

MRI or defecography demonstrates cystocele, rectocele, enterocele, or prolapse during straining. The pubococcygeal line is often used as a reference point.
