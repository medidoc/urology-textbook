Bladder Diverticulum: Etiology, Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition

A bladder diverticulum is an outpouching of the bladder wall (Powell et al., 2009). In pseudodiverticula, only the bladder mucosa herniates, and the diverticulum wall lacks a muscular layer. In true diverticula, the outpouching consists of all layers of the bladder wall.

Ultrasound imaging of a small bladder diverticulum.

Epidemiology

Acquired diverticula occur predominantly in men over 50 years of age, usually secondary to chronic subvesical obstruction. Congenital bladder diverticula are rare and usually affect boys younger than ten years.

Etiology

Congenital bladder diverticula:

These are caused by congenital weakness of the bladder wall, most often near the ureteral orifice (Hutch diverticulum). They may also occur with urachal anomalies at the bladder dome. See also the sections on urachal anomalies and Hutch diverticulum. Vesicoureteral reflux is more common with trigonal bladder diverticula, and the ureter may drain directly into the diverticulum. Some congenital bladder diverticula are true diverticula, with herniation of all layers of the bladder wall.

Acquired bladder diverticula:

These are usually caused by chronically increased intravesical pressure, which leads to herniation of the bladder mucosa through gaps in the muscular wall (pseudodiverticula). A pseudocapsule forms around the diverticulum and facilitates its resection. The diverticulum often has a narrow, sphincter-like neck, which increases urinary stasis within it.

  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia: affects older men, usually over 50 years of age.
  • Neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction, especially with detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia.
  • Urethral valves: affect boys.
  • Iatrogenic causes, such as inadequate suturing of the bladder.

Clinical Presentation

Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), which may also be caused by the underlying disease, recurrent urinary tract infections, and bladder stones within the diverticulum.

Diagnosis

Urine:

Obtain a urine culture before surgery.

Ultrasound imaging:

Bladder diverticula can be reliably identified when the bladder is full [fig. Ultrasound imaging of a small bladder diverticulum and ultrasound imaging of a large diverticulum]. Examine the kidneys to exclude hydronephrosis or identify a duplex kidney.

Ultrasound imaging of a large bladder diverticulum in a patient with subvesical obstruction (sagittal plane, left; transverse plane, right): the diverticulum is considerably larger than the bladder (HB), with marked detrusor wall thickening and a narrow diverticulum neck. Voiding cystourethrography:

Obtain lateral and anteroposterior images to document the size and location of the diverticulum accurately [fig. Cystogram of a bladder diverticulum]. The extent of filling that remains in the diverticulum after voiding is important for assessment.

Cystogram of a bladder diverticulum before surgery: a catheter was inserted into the diverticulum and its balloon inflated with 50 ml. A ureteral stent was placed beforehand because of the proximity of the ureteral orifice. The left-sided diverticulum displaces the ureter and bladder to the right.

Unopened postoperative specimen with an inflated catheter balloon.

Cystoscopy:

Cystoscopy is used to examine the bladder and diverticulum mucosa [fig. cystoscopic findings of BPH and a bladder diverticulum]. Biopsy suspicious areas (caution: the diverticulum wall is thin). Document the anatomical relationship between the diverticulum neck and ureteral orifice. Examine the prostate and urethra for subvesical obstruction.

Cystoscopic findings of BPH, bladder diverticulum, and trabeculated bladder wall.

Cystoscopic findings of a bladder diverticulum with bladder carcinoma. With kind permission, Dr. J. Schönebeck, Ljungby, Sweden.

Imaging of the upper urinary tract:

Renal ultrasound is usually sufficient. If findings are abnormal, CT urography or retrograde pyelography may be necessary. Conventional intravenous urography is considered obsolete.

Urodynamics:

Urodynamic studies may be indicated for unclear voiding disorders or to rule out detrusor underactivity before surgical treatment.

Treatment of Bladder Diverticulum

Conservative therapy:

Asymptomatic bladder diverticula do not require treatment. In patients at high surgical risk, significant diverticula associated with subvesical obstruction may be managed with clean intermittent self-catheterization or, if this is not feasible, an indwelling catheter.

Endoscopic therapy:

Borderline significant bladder diverticula may be treated endoscopically during relief of subvesical obstruction by incising the diverticulum neck. The aim is to resect the narrow neck to improve communication with the bladder; the entire diverticulum mucosa is also coagulated to induce scarring and shrinkage. Evidence for endoscopic treatment of bladder diverticula is limited.

Surgical therapy:

Various surgical techniques for bladder diverticulectomy have been described; see also the section on bladder diverticulectomy. Placement of a ureteral stent before surgery may be useful.

Open transvesical diverticulectomy:

Suitable for small bladder diverticula, particularly when performed together with transvesical prostate enucleation.

Extravesical diverticulectomy:

Suitable for large bladder diverticula. The procedure may be performed by open surgery, laparoscopy, or robot-assisted surgery, depending on whether simultaneous relief of prostatic obstruction or ureteral reimplantation is required. See also laparoscopy in urology. Placement of a ureteral stent and a transurethral catheter in the diverticulum facilitates orientation during surgery.

Prognosis

Bladder diverticula are associated with an increased risk of urothelial carcinoma (diverticular carcinoma), which is attributed to chronic urinary stasis.
