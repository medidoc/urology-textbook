TNM-Stages, Diagnosis and Treatment of Female Urethral Cancer

Female urethral carcinoma is a rare malignant tumor of the urethra, occurring most often in women over 70 years of age. Primary urethral carcinoma is defined as a first manifestation in the urethra without involvement of other parts of the urinary tract. Secondary urethral carcinoma occurs as a recurrence after diagnosis and treatment of bladder carcinoma or carcinoma of the upper urinary tract; see the section on bladder carcinoma. EAU guidelines: Urethral carcinoma. For men, see male urethral cancer.

Epidemiology

Female urethral carcinoma is very rare, with an annual incidence of 1.5 per million inhabitants. Approximately half of patients already have advanced tumor stage at diagnosis.

Etiology

Risk factors include chronic inflammation or infection, urethral diverticula, caruncles, papillomas, adenomas, leukoplakia, and urothelial carcinoma of the bladder.

Pathology

Histology

Urothelial carcinoma is the most common histologic type (45 %), followed by adenocarcinoma (29 %) and squamous cell carcinoma (19 %). Melanoma is rare (Derksen et al., 2013).

Metastasis

Tumors of the distal urethra metastasize to the inguinal lymph nodes, whereas tumors of the proximal urethra metastasize to the pelvic lymph nodes. The boundaries between these drainage territories are not well defined. Hematogenous metastases occur relatively late in squamous cell carcinoma compared with urothelial carcinoma.

TNM Tumor Stages [UICC TNM 9th Edition, 2025]

T: Local Tumor Stage

  • Ta: Non-invasive papillary carcinoma.
  • Tis: Flat high-grade lesion without invasion.
  • T1: Invasion of the subepithelial connective tissue.
  • T2: Invasion of the periurethral muscles.
  • T3: Invasion of the anterior vaginal wall or bladder neck.
  • T4: Invasion of adjacent organs, such as the rectum or bladder.

N: Regional Lymph Node Involvement

  • N0: No regional lymph node metastases.
  • N1: Metastasis in a single regional lymph node.
  • N2: Metastases in multiple regional lymph nodes.

M: Distant Metastases

  • M0: No distant metastases.
  • M1: Distant metastases.

G: Grading of Adenocarcinoma or Squamous Cell Carcinoma

  • G1: Well differentiated.
  • G2: Moderately differentiated.
  • G3: Poorly differentiated to undifferentiated.

G: Grading of Urothelial Carcinoma

  • Low-grade: Corresponds broadly to the former G1 category and part of G2.
  • High-grade: Corresponds broadly to parts of the former G2 category and G3.

Signs and Symptoms

The main symptoms include urethral bleeding, dysuria and urinary frequency, urinary retention, dyspareunia, and a palpable urethral tumor or local induration on pelvic examination.

Diagnosis

Initial evaluation includes urethrocystoscopy with biopsy or transurethral resection. For invasive tumors, MRI of the pelvis is superior to CT for assessing local extent. Systemic staging is performed with CT of the abdomen and chest.

Treatment

Local excision or resection

Local excision or resection is suitable for well-differentiated, non-invasive tumors of the distal urethra.

Complete urethrectomy with bladder neck closure

This procedure is indicated for invasive tumors without invasion of the bladder. Urinary diversion is performed using either an appendicovesicostomy or a suprapubic indwelling catheter.

Anterior exenteration with complete urethrectomy

This approach includes radical cystectomy and is an option for proximal invasive tumors. Only heterotopic urinary diversion is possible.

Lymphadenectomy

In invasive proximal tumors, lymphadenectomy is performed as for bladder carcinoma. For distal invasive urethral carcinoma, inguinal lymphadenectomy is additionally indicated when there is clinical suspicion of lymph node involvement.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy

Randomized studies are lacking. Treatment decisions are based on histology, by analogy with urothelial or squamous cell carcinoma. For locally advanced urothelial carcinoma, platinum-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy is standard.

Radiochemotherapy

Neoadjuvant radiochemotherapy achieves a high response rate (80 %), particularly in squamous cell carcinoma, so an organ-preserving approach without curative resection may be considered (Kent et al., 2015). Adjuvant radiochemotherapy after curative resection of advanced tumors is also an option, particularly for patients with positive surgical margins or lymph node metastases.

Chemotherapy for metastatic urethral carcinoma

Studies specific to urethral carcinoma are lacking. By analogy, chemotherapy is selected according to the underlying histology; see also the section on chemotherapy for metastatic bladder carcinoma.

Prognosis

The 5-year survival rate for patients with localized tumors is nearly 70 %. It is approximately 50 % for patients with advanced tumors and 17 % for those with metastatic disease (Derksen et al., 2013).
