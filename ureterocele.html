Ureterocele: Symptoms, Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition

A ureterocele is a cystic dilatation of the distal, intravesical ureter [fig. ureterocele cross-section]. In 80 % of cases, the ureterocele drains the upper pole of a duplex kidney [fig. ureterocele with duplex kidney]. The defect in the bladder wall caused by a ureterocele may lead to vesicoureteral reflux into the lower pole of a duplex kidney.

For treatment decisions, the most important distinction is between intravesical (orthotopic) and extravesical (ectopic) ureteroceles. Other relevant factors include obstruction, reflux, and whether the ureterocele is associated with a single or duplex system.

Epidemiology

The prevalence is 1:5000. Ureteroceles are more common in females than in males (4:1). Approximately 10 % are bilateral.

Etiology

Several theories have been proposed, but none explains all forms of ureterocele.

Chwalla membrane

The Chwalla membrane separates the urogenital sinus from the ureteral bud. The stenotic ureteral orifice in ureteroceles has been attributed to delayed and incomplete reabsorption of the Chwalla membrane, which may also lead to cystic dilatation of the intravesical ureter. This theory does not explain the different sizes of ureteroceles.

Disturbed fusion of the Wolffian duct with the urogenital sinus

This theory links ureterocele formation with ureteral ectopia: the more lateral the position of the ureteral bud, the later and more disturbed its fusion with the urogenital sinus.

Acquired ureteroceles

In rare cases, stenosis of the ureteral orifice caused by inflammation, a ureteral stone, or trauma can lead to cystic dilatation of the distal intramural ureter.

Signs and Symptoms

Ureteroceles are often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on imaging. Possible symptoms include urinary tract infection, hematuria, incontinence, urethral prolapse, urinary retention, hydronephrosis, pyelonephritis, urosepsis, nephrolithiasis, or a ureterocele stone [ureterocele stone]. Very rarely, obstruction causes a palpable abdominal mass in a child.

Diagnosis

Beyond ultrasound imaging, further diagnostic evaluation is indicated only for patients with symptoms, urinary tract obstruction, suspected reflux, impaired renal function, or planned treatment.

Ultrasound imaging

Ureteroceles appear as thin-walled cysts in the bladder [fig. ureterocele on transrectal ultrasound]. They are best visualized when the bladder is moderately filled. A ureterocele may cause obstruction of the contralateral kidney. A duplex kidney with hydronephrosis of the upper pole may also be identified.

Transrectal ultrasound imaging of a ureterocele (*) in the filled bladder (HB). An enlarged seminal vesicle (SB) is also seen. With kind permission, Dr. med. H. Kempter, Augsburg.

Voiding cystourethrography

Voiding cystourethrography is indicated to evaluate voiding symptoms, vesicoureteral reflux, or suspected intraurethral prolapse. The ureterocele appears as a filling defect that shows its size and location; it is best visualized during early bladder filling. When the bladder is full, the ureterocele may collapse and prolapse into its ureter, making it appear like a bladder diverticulum. Vesicoureteral reflux into the lower pole is found in up to 50 % of duplex kidneys.

Cystoscopy

Cystoscopy is indicated when the anatomy is unclear, when voiding symptoms are present, or before planned endoscopic treatment. See fig. ureterocele at cystoscopy and ureterocele with a stone.

Retrograde pyelography

Retrograde pyelography is indicated when the anatomy is unclear and before surgical treatment. See fig. retrograde pyelography and ureterocele with a stone.

Renal scintigraphy

Renal scintigraphy determines differential renal function and, in duplex kidneys, the function of the renal moiety drained by the ureterocele. DMSA scintigraphy is useful for assessing parenchymal function and scarring; MAG3 scintigraphy evaluates drainage.

Intravenous urography

Intravenous urography has largely been replaced by MR urography or CT urography. Typical findings include poor visualization of the upper renal moiety due to impaired function, although it may opacify on delayed images. A reduced number of visible calyces and greater distance between the renal unit and the spine suggest a nonopacified upper moiety. The lower pole ureter follows a tortuous course. The ureterocele may sometimes be seen in the bladder [fig. ureterocele on urography and opacified ureterocele]. The distended end of an intravesical, orthotopic ureterocele is also called the cobra head sign.

Treatment

An asymptomatic ureterocele without significant obstruction, high-grade reflux, or relevant loss of renal function does not require immediate surgery. It should be monitored with ultrasound and, depending on the findings, additional imaging.

Transurethral ureterocele incision

Ureterocele incision is a treatment option with low morbidity [fig. transurethral incision of a ureterocele]. It is often curative for an obstructive intravesical ureterocele in a single system or in a duplex system without severe reflux into the lower pole. Although extravesical ureteroceles are less likely to be cured by incision, incision is often attempted as the initial treatment. If obstruction recurs or severe postoperative reflux develops, one of the procedures described below may be required as a second-stage operation.

Transurethral incision of a ureterocele followed by removal of multiple ureterocele stones.

Ureteropyelostomy or ureteroureterostomy

These procedures are indicated for an obstructive ureterocele (recurrence after transurethral incision) in a duplex system with salvageable renal function and without severe reflux into the lower pole. The upper pole ureter is usually anastomosed end-to-side to the lower pole renal pelvis or ureter. Surgery can be performed by an open, laparoscopic, retroperitoneoscopic, or robot-assisted approach. The ureterocele should decompress spontaneously, thereby reducing reflux into the lower pole. If severe reflux persists, ureterocele resection and ureteral reimplantation of the lower pole ureter are required as a second-stage procedure.

Heminephrectomy and partial ureterectomy

These procedures are indicated for a ureterocele in a duplex system with poor upper pole function and without severe reflux into the lower pole. Surgery can be performed by an open, laparoscopic, retroperitoneoscopic, or robot-assisted approach. The ureterocele should decompress spontaneously, thereby reducing reflux into the lower pole. If severe reflux persists, a second-stage procedure is required: ureterocele resection and ureteral reimplantation of the lower pole ureter.

Simultaneous reconstruction of the upper and lower urinary tract

Simultaneous reconstruction may be necessary for a ureterocele in a duplex system with severe reflux (grade III or higher) or for a complicated extravesical ureterocele. The upper urinary tract is treated with heminephrectomy or ureteropyelostomy/ureteroureterostomy. Through a separate approach to the bladder, the ureterocele is excised and the lower pole ureter is reimplanted.
