Ureteropelvic Junction (UPJ Obstruction): Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition of UPJ Obstruction

Ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) obstruction is usually a congenital malformation that obstructs urine flow where the ureter joins the renal pelvis. Synonyms include ureteropelvic junction stenosis and UPJ obstruction. See the anatomy of the ureter. Congenital UPJ obstruction is a common cause of hydronephrosis (Tan and Smith, 2004).

Schematic drawing of a left-sided UPJ obstruction caused by a lower pole renal artery. After surgical correction using the Anderson-Hynes technique, the ureteral anastomosis is positioned anterior to the vessel (right). With kind permission, S. Hoffmeister, Stadtbergen.

Epidemiology of UPJ Obstruction

Neonatal period

UPJ obstruction accounts for up to 40 % of cases of fetal hydronephrosis.

Postnatal data

The incidence is 1:1,500. The obstruction is left-sided in 60 % of cases and bilateral in 10–40 %. The male-to-female ratio is 2:1.

Etiology of UPJ Obstruction

Intrinsic causes

Abnormal development of the smooth muscle, altered innervation, and decreased peristalsis of the proximal ureter are suspected. Rare intrinsic causes include ureteral valves and ureteral polyps.

Extrinsic causes

  • Crossing lower pole vessels can compress the UPJ anteriorly or kink the junction between the renal pelvis and proximal ureter [fig. intraoperative findings of a UPJ obstruction]. Crossing lower pole vessels are particularly common in laparoscopic and adult surgical series (50 %).
  • Common associated malformations include pelvic kidney, horseshoe kidney, renal malrotation, and duplex kidney.

Secondary UPJ obstruction

UPJ obstruction may be associated with high-grade vesicoureteral reflux.

Pathophysiology of UPJ Obstruction

Increased resistance to urine flow

Obstruction causes a chronic increase in pressure within the renal pelvis and calyces, resulting in dilatation and smooth muscle hypertrophy of the pyelocalyceal system and increased intratubular pressure.

RAAS activation and loss of renal function

Chronic obstruction activates the intrarenal renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). Angiotensin II reduces renal perfusion and promotes tubulointerstitial fibrosis through transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), inflammation, and extracellular matrix formation. Thus, renal functional loss in UPJ obstruction results not only from mechanical pressure but also from chronic obstructive nephropathy with tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis.

Signs and Symptoms of UPJ Obstruction

Newborns and infants

An abdominal mass, failure to thrive, and urosepsis were typical presentations in the past. Today, many children are diagnosed by prenatal ultrasound and are initially asymptomatic.

Children and adults

Flank or upper abdominal pain, often with nausea and vomiting, may occur, particularly after fluid intake (Dietl crisis). Pyelonephritis, hematuria, and nephrolithiasis may also occur.

Diagnosis of UPJ Obstruction

Ultrasound

A typical finding on renal ultrasound is hydronephrosis with dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces and no dilatation of the proximal ureter. Findings that may indicate chronic parenchymal damage include progressive hydronephrosis, increasing calyceal dilatation, parenchymal thinning, reduced corticomedullary differentiation, and increased parenchymal echogenicity. Ultrasound alone cannot establish functionally significant obstruction or loss of renal function. Renal scintigraphy is indicated when dilatation progresses or ultrasound shows signs of parenchymal damage.

The anteroposterior diameter of the renal pelvis and the ultrasound-assessed severity of calyceal dilatation are used for follow-up. Renal scintigraphy should be considered if dilatation persists or progresses, particularly when the anteroposterior diameter exceeds 12 mm after the first few months of life. The diameter of the renal pelvis is also a risk factor for subsequent surgery: children with an anteroposterior diameter greater than 20 mm have an increased risk of requiring surgery.

Renal scintigraphy

Renal scintigraphy with a radionuclide (MAG3) measures differential renal function and assesses drainage from the renal pelvis. If drainage is delayed, furosemide is administered to assess tracer washout from the pyelocalyceal system. A tracer washout half-time of less than 10 min argues against significant obstruction, whereas a half-time greater than 20 min indicates significant obstruction. Intermediate values must be interpreted during follow-up. Indications for surgery include reduced differential renal function (<40 %), declining function over time, poor drainage after furosemide, or increasing hydronephrosis.

Voiding cystourethrography

Voiding cystourethrography is indicated when vesicoureteral reflux is suspected, particularly if the ureter is dilated.

Retrograde pyelography

Retrograde pyelography is indicated for unclear proximal ureteral strictures before diagnostic ureteroscopy or placement of a double-J stent before surgery [fig. retrograde pyelography in a duplex system and retrograde pyelography of left-sided UPJ obstruction].

CT urography or MR urography

These studies provide information about anatomy, excretion, renal function, and crossing lower pole vessels. Cross-sectional imaging is particularly useful in adults, when anatomy is unclear or stones are suspected, and before planned endopyelotomy to identify or exclude a lower pole vessel.

Intravenous urography

Findings include dilatation of the pyelocalyceal system, a high and stenotic ureteral insertion, and delayed excretion of contrast medium. Intravenous urography is no longer indicated in children; when imaging is required, MR urography is used.

Whitaker test

The Whitaker test measures pressure in the renal pelvis and bladder during infusion through a nephrostomy at 10 ml/min. A pressure difference greater than 20 cm of water is abnormal. Because the test is invasive, it is rarely used in routine practice.

Differential Diagnosis of UPJ Obstruction

See the section on hydronephrosis. In children, consider megacalycosis, high-grade vesicoureteral reflux, and megaureter.

Treatment of UPJ Obstruction

Active surveillance

In the absence of symptoms, with stable or decreasing hydronephrosis and preserved differential renal function, active surveillance with regular ultrasound follow-up is standard. Renal scintigraphy should be repeated during follow-up when dilatation is severe, persistent, or progressive.

Indications for surgery

  • Significant obstruction on renal scintigraphy
  • Decreasing differential renal function on scintigraphy (<40 %)
  • Symptomatic obstruction with recurrent flank pain, pyelonephritis, or nephrolithiasis.

Pyeloplasty

The Anderson-Hynes dismembered pyeloplasty is the most commonly used technique for treating UPJ obstruction and can be performed through an open approach, laparoscopically, retroperitoneoscopically, or with robotic assistance (Munver et al., 2004). The technique involves transection and reduction of the renal pelvis, excision of the narrowed segment, spatulation of the proximal ureter, and reanastomosis. If a lower pole vessel is present, the anastomosis is placed anterior to the vessel after transection [fig. UPJ obstruction]. For the surgical technique and complications, see open pyeloplasty or laparoscopic pyeloplasty.

Endopyelotomy

The UPJ is accessed endoscopically in a retrograde (ureteroscopy) or antegrade manner, and the stenosis is incised posteriorly, usually at the 6 o'clock position under retrograde vision, using a cold knife or laser fiber. Endopyelotomy is primarily an option for adults after a crossing lower pole vessel has been excluded, and for recurrent strictures after failed pyeloplasty. Its success rate is lower than that of pyeloplasty, particularly with a long stricture, severe hydronephrosis, poor renal function, or a crossing vessel. Endopyelotomy is rarely performed in children. Disadvantages include radiation exposure, the need for a stent, possible repeat anesthesia, and a lower success rate than pyeloplasty.

Nephrectomy

Nephrectomy may be considered when differential function of the affected kidney is poor (<10–20 % of total renal function), particularly in patients with symptoms, infections, hypertension, or nonfunctioning hydronephrosis. It can usually be performed using a minimally invasive approach (laparoscopic or retroperitoneoscopic nephrectomy).
