Urinary Bladder Stones – Bladder Calculi: Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition

Bladder stones are a common form of urolithiasis of the lower urinary tract.

Abdominal radiograph with bladder calculi (above). The markedly enlarged prostate displaces the stones cranially. Specimen of the same bladder stones after surgical removal (below). With kind permission, Prof. Dr. R. Harzmann, Augsburg.

Epidemiology and Etiology

Endemic Bladder Stones

Malnutrition in developing countries causes bladder stones in children without neurologic disorders or bladder emptying disorders. Affected regions include North Africa, the Middle East, and the Far East. Boys younger than ten years are affected more often because girls can pass sandy precursors more easily through their shorter urethra.

In children, bladder stone formation is associated with a diet based mainly on cereals and low in animal protein. Other contributing factors include dehydration and low dietary phosphate intake. Pediatric bladder stones most commonly consist of ammonium urate, with or without calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate.

Secondary Bladder Stones

In developed countries, secondary bladder stones predominate and account for approximately 5 % of urolithiasis. Risk factors include urinary stasis, recurrent urinary tract infections, foreign bodies, and intestinal mucosa in the urinary tract (urinary diversion or augmentation).

Subvesical Obstruction

Postvoid residual urine (urinary stasis), often caused by benign prostatic hyperplasia or neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction, is the most common cause of bladder stones (75 %). These stones consist of uric acid, calcium oxalate, or magnesium ammonium phosphate (infection stones). They affect mainly men over 50 years of age.

Urinary Tract Infections and Catheterization

Recurrent or chronic urinary tract infections with urease-producing bacteria lead to magnesium ammonium phosphate stones. Compared with catheter-free patients with spinal cord injury, those with indwelling bladder catheters have a ninefold risk of bladder stones. Intermittent self-catheterization or condom catheter drainage is associated with a fourfold increased risk.

Foreign Bodies

Self-introduced, iatrogenic, or migratory foreign bodies in contact with urine can cause bladder stones. Iatrogenic causes include suture material, clips, catheters, and ureteral stents. Intrauterine devices and other foreign bodies may migrate transmurally into the bladder.

Urinary Diversion

The incidence of urinary stone formation (4–48 %) depends on the type of urinary diversion. Stones occur three times more often with continent than with incontinent urinary diversions. Risk factors include urinary stasis, mucus production, urinary tract infections, foreign material (clips and nonabsorbable suture material), catheterization, and metabolic disorders. The stones are usually magnesium ammonium phosphate (infection stones).

Signs and Symptoms

Childhood Symptoms in Endemic Bladder Stones

Symptoms include abdominal pain, an interrupted urinary stream, handling or pulling at the penis, dysuria, alguria, and frequency. Symptoms usually persist for a prolonged period.

Symptoms Due to Secondary Bladder Stones

Most bladder stones cause no additional symptoms beyond those of the underlying bladder emptying disorder. Typical symptoms include recurrent urinary tract infections, dysuria, alguria, an interrupted urinary stream, or acute urinary retention.

Complications

Urosepsis, painful impaction in the urethra, urinary retention with postrenal kidney failure, and, rarely, squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder.

Diagnostic Workup of Bladder Calculi

Ultrasound Imaging

Bladder stones appear as echogenic masses in the bladder with posterior acoustic shadowing. They are reliably detected when the bladder is full [fig. ultrasound imaging of a bladder stone]. After repositioning the patient, the stones should move with gravity. See also bladder ultrasound.


Ultrasound imaging of a bladder stone: With kind permission, Prof. Dr. R. Harzmann, Augsburg.

Abdominal Radiograph

Many stones are radiopaque [fig. abdominal radiograph with bladder calculi], but some are obscured by overlying bowel gas or are radiolucent.

Cystography

Radiolucent bladder stones can be detected as filling defects on cystography due to the contrast medium [fig. cystography with bladder stones].


Cystography showing multiple diverticula: the right diverticulum is filled with a bladder stone, as shown by the double contrast. With kind permission, Dr. R. Gumpinger, Kempten.

Computed Tomography

Even without contrast medium, computed tomography has the highest sensitivity for imaging bladder stones.

Cystoscopy

Cystoscopy is a reliable method for diagnosing bladder stones and is important for treatment planning, including assessment of prostate size, bladder diverticula, and urethral strictures. See figs. stone punch and electrohydraulic lithotripsy.





Treatment of Urinary Bladder Stones

Most bladder stones can be treated endoscopically. The anatomy, stone etiology, comorbidities, and stone size influence the choice of procedure.

Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL)

ESWL is a treatment option for children or patients at high risk from anesthesia. Without treatment of the underlying cause, the risk of recurrence is high.

Transurethral Lithotripsy

Options include mechanical, electrohydraulic, pneumatic, or laser lithotripsy (see technique and complications of transurethral cystolitholapaxy). Depending on the intraoperative course, surgical treatment of benign prostatic hyperplasia, if indicated, may be performed during the same session.

Percutaneous Cystolithotomy

This procedure is indicated in children to protect the urethra or in patients with a high stone burden. After percutaneous bladder puncture and insertion of a rigid guidewire, an access tract of 15–24 Ch is dilated. As in percutaneous nephrolithotomy, the stones are fragmented and removed.

Open Cystolithotomy (Sectio Alta)

The choice between endoscopic lithotripsy and open cystolithotomy depends on the size and number of stones and on prostate size, particularly when simultaneous open surgery for benign prostatic hyperplasia is planned. See the section on surgical treatment of benign prostatic hyperplasia for related information. In experienced centers, open cystolithotomy may be replaced by robot-assisted simple prostatectomy with stone removal. Clinical benefits are expected only with an extraperitoneal robotic approach. A transperitoneal approach may entail unnecessary risks of serious complications, such as ileus, urinary ascites, or adhesions.

Endemic Bladder Stones

In addition to surgical treatment, a consistent dietary change, including increased animal protein and a balanced mixed diet, helps prevent recurrence.
