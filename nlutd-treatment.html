Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction: Diagnosis and Treatment

Diagnosis of NLUTD

History

A voiding diary helps quantify voiding symptoms. Ask about prior surgery, neurological symptoms, medications, bowel function, urinary tract infections, catheterization, sexual function, and functional limitations to aid the differential diagnosis.

Physical examination

Perform a urological and gynecological examination and assess for stress incontinence. A focused neurological examination includes sensory testing of each (sacral) dermatome, muscle strength, biceps, Achilles, and patellar tendon reflexes, the Babinski sign, anal reflex, bulbospongiosus reflex, cremasteric reflex, and anal sphincter tone.

Absent reflex response

May occur with acute first motor neuron injury (acute spinal cord syndrome) or peripheral nerve injury (herniated disk, nerve transection).

Increased reflex activity

May occur with chronic first motor neuron injury (spinal cord syndrome, stroke). Other signs of pyramidal tract injury include a positive Babinski sign.

Laboratory tests

Urinalysis, urine culture if urinary tract infection is suspected, and serum creatinine. Estimate the glomerular filtration rate in patients at risk.

Ultrasound

Assess for hydronephrosis, renal parenchymal thickness, residual urine, bladder wall thickness, bladder stones, and diverticula. See bladder ultrasound.

Cystoscopy

Cystoscopy is less useful for diagnosing the neurogenic component than for excluding differential diagnoses such as tumors, cystitis, bladder stones, foreign bodies, or urethral pathology. See cystoscopy.

Voiding cystourethrography

Usually performed as part of urodynamic testing. Relevant abnormal findings include reduced bladder capacity, multiple small diverticula (Christmas tree bladder; see Figure 2), residual urine, signs of bladder outlet obstruction, or vesicoureteral reflux.

Urodynamics

The type of dysfunction cannot be reliably predicted from clinical symptoms, but not every patient requires urodynamic testing. Testing is useful when conservative treatment has failed, in neurological diseases associated with a risk of DSD and high voiding pressures, and before invasive treatment. See the referenced urodynamic findings in Figures 1 and 2.

Differential Diagnosis

Overactive bladder, dysfunctional voiding, benign prostatic hyperplasia, bladder tumors, bladder stones, infections, and medication-induced voiding dysfunction.

Treatment – Improving Bladder Storage Function

Behavioral therapy

Indications include mild urgency symptoms or mild urgency incontinence. Treatment includes adjusting fluid intake, scheduled voiding, bladder training, and pelvic floor therapy, with electrical stimulation if appropriate, often combined with medication.

Anticholinergic therapy

Anticholinergics act as antagonists at muscarinic receptors, causing relaxation of smooth muscle. In the bladder, they reduce the force of normal or involuntary detrusor contractions and increase functional bladder capacity.

Regular voiding is needed for substantial symptomatic improvement. The goal is to void before bladder volume reaches the threshold that triggers an involuntary contraction. Bothersome adverse effects often lead patients to discontinue treatment. The following drugs are commonly used:

  • Trospium: up to 15 mg 1-1-1.
  • Oxybutynin: 2.5 mg 1-0-1 to 5 mg 1-1-1-1.
  • Tolterodine: 1 mg 1-0-0 to 2 mg 1-1-1. Extended-release formulation: 4 mg 1-0-0.
  • Fesoterodine: 4–8 mg 1-0-0.
  • Propiverine: 15 mg 1-0-1, with an increase to 1-1-1 if needed.
  • Darifenacin: 7.5–15 mg 1-0-0.
  • Solifenacin: 5–10 mg 1-0-0.

Intravesical anticholinergic therapy

Intravesical oxybutynin is used mainly in patients with spina bifida, spinal cord injury, or other neurogenic detrusor overactivity who already perform intermittent catheterization. The goals are to lower detrusor pressure, increase functional bladder capacity, reduce urgency incontinence episodes, and protect the upper urinary tract. The daily dose of 10–40 mg is divided into 2–4 instillations; the drug remains in the bladder until the next emptying. Approved only in the European Union and the United Kingdom.

Adrenergic beta-3 receptor agonists

Adrenergic beta-3 receptors are also present in the bladder and have minimal cardiovascular effects. Selective beta-3 receptor agonists, such as mirabegron and vibegron, have shown in clinical studies to be treatment options with few adverse effects for improving bladder storage function. Mirabegron is approved to treat neurogenic detrusor overactivity in children aged three years and older. Use in adults with neurogenic detrusor overactivity is off-label, but many guidelines recommend it as an alternative to anticholinergics. The mirabegron dose is 50 mg 1-0-0 p.o. Reduce the dose to 25 mg 1-0-0 p.o. in severe renal insufficiency or moderate hepatic insufficiency. The vibegron dose is 75 mg once daily.

Botulinum toxin A

Transurethral injections of botulinum toxin A into the detrusor cause temporary detrusor weakness lasting several months and increase functional bladder capacity. Botulinum toxin A is indicated when oral therapy fails or is not tolerated. The dose (e.g., 100–300 units of Botox) must be titrated to effect and adverse effects, particularly in patients who void spontaneously. Excessive dosing may cause residual urine and make intermittent self-catheterization necessary. See botulinum toxin for pharmacology and adverse effects.

Sacral neuromodulation

Electrical stimulation of the S3 dorsal root can modulate the micturition reflex and improve storage or emptying symptoms in selected patients.

Indication

Neurogenic detrusor overactivity that responds inadequately to medication. Before implanting the subcutaneous pulse generator, potential treatment success is assessed with several days of percutaneous testing. A permanent pulse generator is implanted in approximately 50–70 % of patients.

Tibial nerve stimulation

Stimulation of afferent fibers of the posterior tibial nerve may modulate the sacral micturition reflex and improve urgency, frequency, and urgency incontinence. Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) uses a needle electrode; transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) uses skin electrodes. Evidence for neurogenic detrusor overactivity is heterogeneous and weaker than for OAB.

Bladder denervation

Bladder denervation includes techniques that interrupt nerves at the spinal or peripheral level. These techniques have fallen out of favor because better alternatives are available. Rare indications include severe autonomic dysreflexia. Adverse effects on other organ systems (rectum, sensation) and nervous system regeneration (neuroplasticity) may result in poorer function.

Bladder augmentation

The goal of bladder augmentation is to increase bladder capacity and lower storage pressures. Good results can be expected when bladder sphincter control is preserved. Residual urine may occur postoperatively, so the patient should be able to perform self-catheterization. Postoperative urinary retention with low filling pressures and the need for self-catheterization is substantially better for the patient than detrusor hyperreflexia with high voiding pressures and a risk of kidney damage. Techniques include:

  • Bladder autoaugmentation with preservation of the urothelium (detrusor myotomy).
  • Bladder augmentation using bowel (stomach, ileum, or colon).
  • Bladder augmentation using a ureter, if a severely dilated ureter from a nonfunctioning kidney is available.

Treatment – Improving Continence

Medication

Duloxetine is a serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor that acts at the spinal level and strengthens sphincter contraction. Because of safety concerns, duloxetine is not approved in the United States. See duloxetine for dosage and adverse effects.

Artificial sphincter

For sphincter insufficiency. An artificial sphincter is not an option in patients with uncontrolled detrusor overactivity, limited manual dexterity, or cognitive impairment that prevents device operation. If inadequate bladder emptying requires self-catheterization, the sphincter cuff should be placed around the bladder neck.

Treatment – Improving Bladder Emptying

Manual compression (Credé maneuver) or Valsalva maneuver

These methods generally produce bladder pressures above 50 cm H2O and may endanger the upper urinary tract.

Manual triggering of the micturition reflex

Usually triggered by tapping the suprapubic bladder region, but often inadequate.

Parasympathomimetics

Stimulation of bladder M receptors increases detrusor contractility. These drugs have been used to treat postoperative urinary retention and neurogenic detrusor weakness. Their practical use is limited by modest efficacy and potential cardiovascular adverse effects.

Bethanechol

10 mg 1-1-1 to 50 mg 1-1-1-1 p.o.; titrate gradually according to response.

Distigmine

Indirect parasympathomimetic that inhibits cholinesterase. Give 0.5 mg i.m. or slowly i.v. For oral administration, give 5 mg 1-0-0 every 2 days, increasing according to response to 1-0-1 daily.

Aseptic intermittent self-catheterization

An effective method for inadequate bladder emptying due to neurogenic dysfunction or after bladder augmentation. In patients performing self-catheterization, detrusor overactivity can be treated with high-dose botulinum toxin or bladder augmentation. Lapides popularized clean intermittent self-catheterization with reuse of cleaned catheters; sterile hydrophilic-coated single-use catheters are now standard in Germany. Complications include urethral injury with false passage, stricture, and urinary tract infection. See also the section on intermittent catheterization.

Indwelling catheterization

Use a urethral catheter in women and a suprapubic catheter in men. Indwelling catheterization has a higher complication rate, including infection, urinary stone formation, gross hematuria, chronic decline in renal function, and increased risk of squamous cell carcinoma with long-term drainage. Depending on age and underlying diseases, intermittent catheterization, a condom catheter, or urinary diversion may be preferable.

Sacral neuromodulation

S3 sacral nerve stimulation may improve detrusor contraction in selected patients with nonobstructive urinary retention. During the storage phase, modulation may reduce involuntary contractions; after modulation is stopped, responders may void more effectively.

Other, non-established treatment options

Bladder reduction surgery, bladder myoplasty, and transurethral electrical bladder stimulation (TEBS) are historical or experimental procedures with no role in routine clinical care.

Treatment – Reducing Bladder Outlet Resistance

Alpha blockers

Indicated for sacral and infrasacral lesions, especially with fixed high sphincter tone or smooth muscle sphincter dyssynergia. See alpha blockers for pharmacology.

Baclofen

Activates GABAB receptors and inhibits monosynaptic or polysynaptic activation of motor neurons. In urology, baclofen may be considered for suprasacral spinal cord lesions with bothersome spasticity, pelvic floor or sphincter spasticity, and urodynamically confirmed DSD. Its effects on bladder emptying and storage pressures vary.

Botulinum toxin A

Transurethral injection into the sphincter and/or detrusor is indicated for DSD and/or detrusor overactivity. See the section on botulinum toxin for pharmacology, dosing, and adverse effects.

Transurethral incision of the prostate (TUIP)

Incisions at the 5- and 7-o'clock positions extend from the bladder neck to the seminal colliculus. A rare indication in men is dysfunctional voiding or detrusor-sphincter dyscoordination.

Endoscopic sphincterotomy

Endoscopic resection or incision of the sphincter at the 12-o'clock position creates stress incontinence and lowers the detrusor leak point pressure (DLPP). After surgery, the patient uses a condom catheter. Sphincterotomy or sphincter dilation is a poor option for women because reliable external drainage systems are unavailable.

The incision must extend from the verumontanum to the bulbomembranous urethra. The DLPP after sphincterotomy should be < 40 cm H2O. The long-term failure rate may reach 50 % because of sphincter regeneration or scar-related strictures. A sphincter stent is an alternative. Close monitoring of the upper urinary tract is necessary.

Treatment – Urinary Diversion

A urinary diversion should be considered when conservative, medical, endoscopic, or reconstructive treatments have failed or are not feasible, for example, with recurrent symptomatic urinary tract infections, upper urinary tract deterioration with renal insufficiency, uncontrollable high bladder pressures, or a permanent need for an indwelling catheter. Options depend on patient preferences, manual dexterity, cognitive function, renal function, and underlying diseases. They include ileovesicostomy, Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy, ileal conduit, and continent heterotopic urinary diversions such as the Mainz pouch I.

Prognosis

Poor prognostic factors include reduced bladder compliance, high storage pressures, detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia, vesicoureteral reflux, recurrent febrile urinary tract infections, high residual urine, and impaired renal function. With low-pressure storage, reliable emptying, and regular monitoring, renal function can usually be preserved long term. High-risk patients have a substantial risk of chronic kidney dysfunction; in patients with spina bifida, the risk may be as high as 50 %.
