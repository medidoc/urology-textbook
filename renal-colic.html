Symptoms and Diagnosis of Nephrolithiasis and Renal Colic

  • Nephrolithiasis: Etiology and Epidemiology
  • Diagnosis of Nephrolithiasis and Renal Colic
  • Treatment of Kidney and Ureteral Stones

Signs and Symptoms

Renal colic:

Renal colic typically presents as sudden flank pain that may be continuous or vary in intensity. Pain radiation depends on the location of the stone.

  • Stone location in the renal pelvis: colicky pain (due to distention of the renal pelvis) or constant flank pain (due to distention of the renal capsule in the presence of obstruction).
  • Stone location in the ureter: colicky or constant flank pain radiating to the lower abdomen.
  • Stone location near the bladder: colicky or constant flank pain radiating to the lower abdomen, scrotum, or labium majus, with hyperesthesia. Urinary frequency may also occur because a prevesical stone irritates the bladder.

Complications of nephrolithiasis:

Impaired urine flow causes urinary obstruction and carries a risk of infected hydronephrosis with urosepsis. Sudden obstruction can cause a forniceal rupture, resulting in urine extravasation (urinoma) when a renal calyx tears at its junction with the renal parenchyma. Obstruction of a calyceal neck by a kidney stone can lead to a renal abscess if infection is present. Persistent obstruction causes loss of kidney function and, depending on contralateral kidney function, may lead to kidney failure.

Diagnosis

Diagnostic Evaluation in Renal Colic

Urine sediment:

Microhematuria, macrohematuria, or crystalluria.

Laboratory tests:

Serum creatinine, uric acid, electrolytes, calcium, complete blood count, and C-reactive protein (CRP) should be measured; coagulation tests may be obtained when indicated.

Ultrasound imaging:

Urinary stones appear as echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing [fig. ultrasound imaging of a lower pole calyceal stone and ultrasound imaging of a renal pelvic calculus]. Ultrasound can detect stones in the kidney and proximal ureter. Prevesical stones can be detected when the bladder is adequately filled [fig. ultrasound imaging of a prevesical ureteral stone]. Urinary obstruction is an indirect sign of ureterolithiasis.

Duplex sonography:

Color Doppler ultrasound can detect the urine jet entering the bladder from the ureteral orifice. An absent jet is an indirect sign of an obstructing ureteral stone. Unilateral elevation of the resistive index (RI) is also an indirect sign of acute obstruction. Color Doppler ultrasound can also help identify calyceal or prevesical stones: the twinkling artifact produces a rapidly changing color Doppler signal behind the echogenic focus [fig. twinkling artifact in the kidney and twinkling artifact due to a prevesical stone].


Conventional ultrasound imaging of a calyceal kidney stone (left) with a twinkling artifact (right).

Twinkling artifact caused by a distal ureteral calculi. With kind permission, Dr. N. Dreger, Wuppertal.

Noncontrast computed tomography (low-dose CT):

Noncontrast CT reliably detects urinary calculi, including with low-dose protocols. CT has several advantages over KUB and intravenous urography for diagnosing renal colic [fig. noncontrast CT of a ureteral stone and hydronephrosis]:

  • It can be performed during pain episodes.
  • It avoids contrast medium.
  • It reliably detects nephrolithiasis and urinary obstruction.
  • It can identify important differential diagnoses such as cholelithiasis, perforated peptic ulcer, ileus, and diverticulitis.

One limitation is that CT does not show the anatomy of the collecting system in detail. When evaluating for nephrolithiasis, the radiation dose can be substantially reduced; current techniques require approximately 1 mSv. Another important advantage is that no contrast medium is needed, as increased diuresis caused by contrast medium can lead to forniceal rupture [fig. forniceal rupture on CT].


Noncontrast CT (frontal reconstruction): large left-sided ureteral stone at the level of the iliac vessels with third-degree hydronephrosis. With kind permission, Dr. G. Antes, Kempten.


Abdominal CT with right-sided forniceal rupture: the contrast medium flows around the poorly contrasted renal pelvis. With kind permission, Dr. G. Antes, Kempten.

Intravenous urography:

Intravenous urography is a second-choice option if cross-sectional imaging (see noncontrast CT) is unavailable [fig. distal ureteral stone on IVP and proximal ureteral stone on IVP]. It should not be performed during renal colic because increased diuresis caused by contrast medium may cause more pain, forniceal rupture, and urinoma [fig. forniceal rupture on IVP and forniceal rupture on CT]. Urography can show the anatomy of the pyelocalyceal system, which may help guide treatment decisions [fig. staghorn stone].

Signs of urolithiasis:

Most kidney stones appear on a plain radiograph as radiopaque opacities projected over the urinary tract, except for uric acid and xanthine stones. Prompt bilateral excretion of contrast medium indicates good kidney function and argues against urinary obstruction. Delayed contrast medium excretion and dilation of the pyelocalyceal system are signs of obstruction. Delayed images obtained 1–2 h after contrast medium administration often show the ureter opacified up to the ureteral stone; radiolucent ureteral stones appear as filling defects. Forniceal rupture with extravasation is rarely seen.

Nephrocalcinosis:

Nephrocalcinosis is a radiologic descriptive diagnosis referring to bilateral calcium crystal deposition in the renal parenchyma and tubular system [fig. nephrocalcinosis]. It is often associated with nephrolithiasis. Plain radiographs show multiple calcifications projected over the kidneys; radiating calcifications correspond to deposits in the collecting ducts. Causes include hypercalciuria (e.g., renal tubular acidosis, hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D intoxication, milk-alkali syndrome, sarcoidosis, tubulopathies, medullary sponge kidney, and other genetic causes of hypercalciuria).


Nephrocalcinosis: bilateral deposition of calcium crystals in the renal parenchyma and tubular system.

Retrograde pyelography:

Retrograde pyelography is indicated when ultrasound or urography findings are inconclusive and before endoscopic treatment.

MRI urography:

MRI urography may be considered in children or pregnant women to avoid radiation exposure, or when indinavir stones are suspected.

Metabolic Evaluation of Nephrolithiasis

Basic workup:

The basic workup is performed in every patient with a first diagnosis of nephrolithiasis.

History:

Stone history, diet, medications, and family history.

Urine tests:

Urine sediment, urine culture, and urine pH.

Serum tests:

Kidney function tests, uric acid, sodium, potassium, and calcium.

Stone analysis:

Analyze passed or removed stones using infrared spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction.

Extended metabolic workup:

An extended workup is indicated in patients at high risk of recurrence (see table risk factors for stone recurrence) or when the basic workup reveals abnormal findings. Ideally, the extended workup should be performed during a stone-free interval.


These risk factors for recurrent nephrolithiasis should lead to an extended metabolic workup (Straub et al., 2005).
High-risk group for recurrent nephrolithiasis
HistoryMore than 3 recurrences in 3 years, children and adolescents, family history with stone formation.
ImagingSingle kidney, residual stone fragments after therapy, nephrocalcinosis, bilateral large stone burden.
Stone typesInfection stones, brushite stones, uric acid stones, xanthine stones.
GenesCystinuria (types A, B, and C), primary hyperoxaluria, renal tubular acidosis type I, APRT (adenine phosphoribosyl transferase) deficiency, xanthine oxidase deficiency, cystic fibrosis, ADPKD.
Other diseasesHyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis, gastrointestinal diseases such as Crohn disease, malabsorption, short bowel syndrome, or colitis.

24-hour urine collection:

Depending on the stone analysis, the following parameters are measured: volume, specific gravity, pH, creatinine, calcium, uric acid, oxalate, citrate, sodium, magnesium, phosphate, ammonium, and cystine.

In patients with hypercalciuria, a repeat 24-hour urine collection may be performed after one week on a low-sodium, low-calcium diet. If calcium excretion falls below 250 mg/24 h, type II (diet-dependent) absorptive hypercalciuria is present. However, distinguishing among the different forms of absorptive hypercalciuria has little clinical value.

Urine pH profile:

Urine pH is measured at each void over the course of one day. Persistently elevated pH values above 7 suggest a bacterial infection with formation of infection stones. Persistently elevated pH values above 5.8 are typical of renal tubular acidosis. Persistently low pH values below 5.8 indicate persistently acidic urine and promote uric acid stone formation.

Serum tests:

Urea, uric acid, creatinine, calcium, phosphate, vitamin D3, and parathyroid hormone.
