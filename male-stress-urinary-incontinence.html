Male Stress Urinary Incontinence: Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition of Stress Urinary Incontinence

During a passive increase in abdominal pressure, bladder pressure exceeds urethral closure pressure, resulting in involuntary urine loss during physical exertion.

Epidemiology

In older men (> 60 years), the prevalence of urinary incontinence is 7–10 % (compared with 10–20 % in older women). Only a minority have pure stress incontinence; urge incontinence and mixed forms are more common.

Etiology

Radical Prostatectomy

The prevalence of urinary incontinence after radical prostatectomy is reported in the literature as 0.5–87 %. This wide range reflects differences in data collection methods, follow-up intervals, and definitions of urinary incontinence. In the long term, about 10 % of patients require more than one pad.

Several causes of urinary incontinence after prostatectomy have been discussed. Direct injury to the external sphincter or its innervation may result from deep sutures in the pelvic floor placed to control bleeding from the Santorini plexus or from tumor infiltration. Protective factors for postoperative continence include a low tumor stage, bilateral nerve-sparing, the length of preserved urethra, younger age, good general health, and no previous prostate surgery, especially TURP.

Postoperative hypermobility of the membranous urethra is considered another factor in sphincter weakness after radical prostatectomy. This finding led to the development of suburethral slings [Gozzi, 2008] and modifications to surgical technique [Rocco, 2009]. Urge incontinence occasionally develops after radical prostatectomy.

TURP and Adenomectomy

Removal of the prostatic adenoma eliminates the prostatic component of the bladder closure mechanism; the urethral pressure profile resembles that of women. Detrusor overactivity also frequently develops postoperatively. Behavioral therapy (adjusting fluid intake and voiding habits), physiotherapy, and anticholinergic medication can help the intact sphincter compensate for these factors [Hampel, 2007].

Persistent urinary incontinence is rare in the long term after surgical treatment of BPH. Causes include persistent urge incontinence, urethral or bladder neck stricture, neurologic disorders, preexisting sphincter insufficiency, or iatrogenic sphincter injury. In the long term, 0.5 % of patients develop stress incontinence due to injury to the external sphincter [Rassweiler, 2006].

Trauma

Distraction injuries of the membranous urethra caused by a pelvic fracture can lead to stress urinary incontinence.

Other Causes

Age, immobility, neurologic disorders, subvesical obstruction, and age-related changes in the urinary tract can cause urinary incontinence in men.

Signs and Symptoms

Stamey Classification

Severity is classified as follows:

  • Grade 1: Urine loss with coughing, sneezing, or laughing.
  • Grade 2: Urine loss while walking or standing up.
  • Grade 3: Urine loss while lying down.

Other Symptoms

Urgency, residual urine, weak urinary stream, urinary retention, and recurrent urinary tract infections.

Diagnosis

History

Important factors include the severity of incontinence, degree of bother, previous surgery, medications (alpha-blockers, clonidine), and neurologic and urologic disorders.

Quantification of Stress Incontinence

A voiding diary documents fluid intake, voided volumes, and incontinence episodes. A period of 24–48 h is usually sufficient. In cooperative patients with a digital scale, incontinence severity can be assessed by weighing the pads.

Pad Test

An alternative to weighing pads as part of the voiding diary is to place a preweighed pad in the underwear. The patient should drink plenty of fluids before the test. The pad is worn for one hour while the patient performs defined provocation exercises, such as climbing stairs, jumping, and coughing; it is then weighed again. Urine loss of more than 25 g is considered severe urinary incontinence.

Urinalysis

Examine the urine sediment and obtain a urine culture if a urinary tract infection is suspected.

Ultrasound Imaging

Ultrasound is used to measure residual urine.

Cystoscopy

Cystoscopy assessment of residual sphincter function is important. Other relevant findings include urethral, bladder neck, and anastomotic strictures.

VCUG and Urodynamics

In clinically clear, uncomplicated stress urinary incontinence without additional symptoms, urodynamics and VCUG may be omitted before primary treatment. Urodynamic testing with VCUG should be performed in patients with mixed urinary incontinence, reduced bladder capacity, bladder emptying disorders, neurologic comorbidities, or before repeat surgery.

Nonoperative Treatment

Incontinence Aids

Pads, absorbent products, and, if needed, a condom catheter provide symptomatic support. An indwelling suprapubic catheter is an option in selected palliative situations or when additional voiding disorders are present.

Physiotherapy

Pelvic floor physiotherapy aims to strengthen the sphincter. Biofeedback can improve exercise performance. Improvement in stress incontinence may occur for up to one year after radical prostatectomy as a result of physiotherapy and the natural course. Randomized trials show an improvement primarily in early continence; the effect on long-term outcomes is smaller [MacDonald, 2007].

Drug Therapy

Anticholinergic medication may relieve symptoms of mixed urinary incontinence. Duloxetine, a serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, specifically targets stress urinary incontinence. Duloxetine is not approved for use in men and may be prescribed only as an "off-label" treatment [Schlenker, 2006]. A trial is appropriate only for mild stress incontinence; numerous contraindications must be considered.

Surgical Treatment

The choice of procedure depends on severity, residual sphincter function, previous surgery, radiation therapy, manual dexterity, patient expectations, and comorbidities. The artificial sphincter has the longest evidence base. Slings are an option primarily for mild to moderate stress incontinence when residual sphincter function is present.

Sphincter Bulking

Urethral bulking agents are not standard treatment for male postprostatectomy stress incontinence because their efficacy is limited and usually not durable. They may be considered in very mild incontinence, when surgery is not feasible, or when temporary symptom relief is desired.

Adjustable Balloon (ProACT)

Two percutaneously placed, adjustable perineal silicone balloons compress the membranous urethra [Hübner, 2007].

Nonadjustable Slings

Bone-anchored systems such as InVance are historical and no longer in common use. Current options primarily include retrourethral transobturator slings such as AdVance and AdVance XP. Residual sphincter function is required for treatment success. In carefully selected patients with mild to moderate stress incontinence, studies show sustained improvement in 60–70 %, although continence rates decline over time [Del Favero et al., 2022].

Adjustable Slings

ATOMS, Argus, and Reemex allow intraoperative or postoperative adjustment of compression. The evidence is heterogeneous, and availability varies by region.

Artificial Sphincter

The artificial sphincter is the treatment of choice when residual sphincter function is absent or other treatment options have failed [fig. AMS 800 components]. Continence outcomes are very good. A major disadvantage is the substantial risk of surgical revision (up to 50 % within 5 years), particularly due to mechanical failure, urethral cuff erosion, and infection [Hussain, 2005]. Patients should have sufficient manual dexterity and cognitive ability to operate the artificial sphincter [fig. AMS 800 control]. The artificial sphincter must be deactivated before transurethral procedures [fig. AMS 800 deactivation].
