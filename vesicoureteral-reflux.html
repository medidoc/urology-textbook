Vesicoureteral Reflux: Classification, Diagnosis, and Treatment

Definition

Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is a common congenital or acquired disorder of the vesicoureteral junction in which urine refluxes into the upper urinary tract. It can lead to recurrent urinary tract infections, pyelonephritis with renal scarring, arterial hypertension, and chronic renal insufficiency. Synonyms include reflux uropathy and vesicorenal reflux. EAU guidelines: Paediatric Urology.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of VUR in asymptomatic children is approximately 1 %. In children with febrile urinary tract infections, the prevalence is substantially higher: up to 50 % in children younger than one year and 15 % in children aged 12 years. VUR is also highly prevalent in children with lower urinary tract dysfunction. The male-to-female ratio is 1:4. VUR tends to run in families.

Etiology

For the anatomy of the bladder trigone and ureteral orifices, see anatomy of the ureterovesical junction.

Primary reflux:

A multifactorial embryologic defect disrupts several developmental steps: premature ureteral budding and fusion of the Wolffian duct with the urogenital sinus, overrotation of the ureteral bud resulting in lateralized orifices, and impaired development of the ureteral and trigonal musculature.

Ureteral duplication:

A duplex system predisposes the ureter of the lower renal pole to reflux because its orifice is usually located more cranially and laterally, and its intramural course is shorter. A coexisting ureterocele of the upper-pole ureter can also disrupt the intramural course.

Secondary vesicoureteral reflux:

Lower urinary tract dysfunction:

Lower urinary tract dysfunction with high voiding pressures (dysfunctional voiding, urethral valves, or neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction, such as that associated with spina bifida) is a major risk factor for high-grade VUR with renal damage.

Decompensation of the ureterovesical junction:

A borderline-functioning ureterovesical junction can decompensate and cause reflux with ascending infection, for example, in cystitis with bladder wall edema or during pregnancy.

Prune-Belly syndrome:

This rare syndrome involves impaired development of the abdominal wall muscles and the smooth muscles of the ureters and bladder. It often causes severe VUR.

Iatrogenic reflux:

Reflux can develop after transection of bladder muscle near the trigone during prostatectomy, transurethral resection of a bladder tumor near the ureteral orifices, or ureterocele resection.

Classification

Earlier classifications distinguished low-pressure reflux during bladder filling from high-pressure reflux during voiding. The current classification by the International Reflux Study Group assigns VCUG findings to grades I–V; see the figure below.

Classification of vesicoureteral reflux based on VCUG findings:
  • Grade I: Reflux into the ureter, not reaching the renal pelvis.
  • Grade II: Reflux into the renal pelvis without dilatation.
  • Grade III: Reflux into the renal pelvis with mild dilatation of the collecting system and no or minimal blunting of the calyces.
  • Grade IV: Reflux with dilatation of the collecting system, mild ureteral kinking, and blunting of the fornices, with papillary impressions still visible.
  • Grade V: Reflux with severe dilatation of the collecting system, ureteral kinking, blunting of the fornices, and loss of papillary impressions.
Schematic drawing of antireflux ureteral reimplantation: Reflux can result from an intramural ureter that is too short (right). After antireflux reimplantation, the ratio of ureteral diameter to tunnel length should be at least 1:5 (left).

Pathophysiology

Insufficient ureterovesical closure mechanism:

This results from a short intramural ureter and a poorly muscularized trigone. The resulting lack of support for the ureter during bladder filling impairs passive reflux protection; active protection through muscle contraction is also inadequate.

Febrile urinary tract infections:

VUR causes urine to shuttle back and forth, which can become infected. Pyelorenal reflux can cause pyelonephritis, which may result in renal scarring, particularly in children.

Papillary anatomy predisposes to pyelorenal reflux, especially at the renal poles. The perpendicular opening of collecting ducts at flat or concave papillae is a risk factor; these papillae are more common at the poles. At convex papillae, collecting ducts open obliquely, making intrarenal reflux less likely.

Reflux nephropathy:

Renal scarring can cause arterial hypertension, proteinuria, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and growth delay in children (see chronic pyelonephritis). Severe disease can progress to end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis. Reflux nephropathy accounts for approximately 10–20 % of chronic kidney disease in children.

Spontaneous resolution:

As the ureter grows and bladder capacity increases, VUR often improves without treatment. Spontaneous resolution is most likely with low-grade reflux, in younger children, with unilateral reflux, an asymptomatic diagnosis, and medially located ureteral orifices.

Clinical Presentation

Acute pyelonephritis:

Fever, chills, flank pain, vomiting, and symptoms of cystitis. Infants and young children may have nonspecific symptoms such as poor feeding, failure to thrive, irritability, or vomiting.

Chronic pyelonephritis:

Impaired physical development in children, hypertension, proteinuria, and, in advanced bilateral disease, symptoms of uremia.

Diagnosis

Ultrasound imaging:

Assess renal parenchymal thickness, parenchymal scars (echogenic areas), the size of both kidneys, and the width of the renal pelvis and proximal ureter to determine disease severity. Renal anatomic variants are more common in these patients. In the lower urinary tract, assess bladder wall thickness, postvoid residual volume, and the diameter of the distal ureter. Ultrasound imaging cannot exclude VUR.

Contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography:

Contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography (ceVUS) avoids ionizing radiation, has high sensitivity, and is suitable as a screening test. After a special gas-filled contrast agent is instilled into the bladder, reflux can be visualized during or after voiding. Its limitations include lack of anatomic visualization of the proximal urethra, which is needed to exclude posterior urethral valves in boys, and of the upper urinary tract, which limits precise grading of reflux.

Voiding cystourethrography:

VCUG is the most important imaging study for detecting VUR [fig. VCUG showing VUR]. In patients with voiding dysfunction, it can also be performed as part of urodynamic testing [fig. Urodynamics and VCUG]. VCUG should be performed only when there is no active infection. It is used to grade VUR (see classification). One diagnostic problem is detecting reflux that is clinically insignificant. In addition, false-negative findings may fail to identify patients with existing renal scars.

Urodynamics and VCUG in a girl with VUR: bilateral grade III–IV reflux caused by detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia. Ballooning of the proximal urethra is visible during voiding. With kind permission, Dr. U. Löffler, Augsburg.

Ureteral diameter ratio (UDR) on VCUG:

The diameter of the distal ureter is divided by the distance between the cranial endplate of L1 and the caudal endplate of L3. This ratio accounts for differences in patient size and imaging magnification and correlates with prognosis (spontaneous resolution, recurrent UTIs, and renal scarring) better than the traditional classification. Low-grade reflux corresponds to a UDR below 0.2. Any increase in UDR substantially worsens prognosis; a UDR above 0.35 makes spontaneous resolution unlikely (Arlen et al., 2017).

Renal scintigraphy:

Static renal scintigraphy with 99mTc-DMSA can reliably detect cortical defects, scars, and acute pyelonephritic changes, as well as assess renal function. If obstruction is suspected, perform functional renal scintigraphy with 99mTc-MAG3.

The diagnostic advantage of DMSA scintigraphy is its ability to reliably identify patients at risk from reflux nephropathy. Some authors recommend early DMSA scintigraphy and forgoing VCUG when the scan is normal. This top-down approach avoids many VCUG examinations but may miss some cases of VUR.

Refluxing ureteral orifice (golf-hole appearance) on cystoscopy. With kind permission, Prof. Dr. R. Harzmann, Augsburg.

Radionuclide cystography:

A sensitive, low-radiation alternative for follow-up examinations, but it depicts bladder and urethral anatomy less clearly than VCUG. In many centers it has been replaced by voiding urosonography.

Workup of neonatal renal pelvic dilatation:

Perform follow-up ultrasound examinations of the kidneys and bladder. VCUG is indicated when there are findings suggestive of clinically significant VUR, such as higher-grade hydronephrosis, ureteral dilatation, a significant ureterocele, an abnormal bladder, cortical abnormalities, or a febrile UTI.

Workup after pyelonephritis:

After a first febrile UTI, renal and bladder ultrasound is sufficient for many children. If the examination is normal, further testing may be unnecessary. After recurrent pyelonephritis or when ultrasound findings are abnormal (especially parenchymal scars, ureteral dilatation, or bladder pathology), VCUG and/or DMSA scintigraphy is indicated (Roberts et al., 2011). Whether to perform VCUG, DMSA scintigraphy, or both remains controversial.

Cystoscopy:

The shape of the ureteral orifices can indicate VUR (horseshoe or golf-hole appearance). The more lateral the orifice, the greater the ureterotrigonal insufficiency and the lower the likelihood of spontaneous resolution. Cystoscopy is not a routine examination for VUR; it is indicated when infravesical obstruction or an anatomic anomaly is suspected, and before endoscopic or surgical reflux correction.

  • Grade 0 (normal): Volcano-shaped orifice with a small opening.
  • Grade 1 (stadium): Oval orifice with a circumferential rim.
  • Grade 2 (horseshoe): Oval orifice with a medially flattened rim.
  • Grade 3 (golf hole): Gaping oval orifice without a rim [fig. golf-hole orifice].

PIC cystogram:

PIC cystography uses a cystoscope to instill contrast medium near the ureteral orifice (PIC, positioning the instillation of contrast). It is more sensitive than conventional VCUG and is an option for children with febrile UTIs and no reflux on standard VCUG (Rubenstein et al., 2003). If reflux is detected, endoscopic injection can be performed during the same session.

Urodynamics:

Indicated when lower urinary tract dysfunction with high voiding pressures is suspected, such as dysfunctional voiding, urethral valves, or neurogenic voiding dysfunction [fig. urodynamic study].

Intravenous urography:

Intravenous urography is rarely indicated for VUR diagnosis today. Possible findings include a dilated (distal) ureter, dilatation of the pyelocalyceal system, signs of healed pyelonephritis (blunted calyces, thin cortex), a duplicated ureter, and an ectopic ureteral orifice. A normal urogram does not exclude reflux.

Conservative Therapy

The goal of VUR treatment is to preserve renal function by preventing pyelonephritis. Many children with primary reflux can be managed conservatively because trigonal insufficiency often resolves as the child grows, and recurrent infections do not occur.

Indications:

Low- to moderate-grade reflux, stable renal function, and controllable UTIs. Initial conservative treatment may also be appropriate for high-grade reflux, depending on age, renal status, symptoms, laterality, and bladder function.

Double voiding:

Scheduled voiding every 3 h. Voiding again a few minutes later empties residual or refluxed urine.

Bacteriuria:

Asymptomatic bacteriuria generally does not require antibiotic treatment. An antibiotic should be prescribed for on-demand use to allow prompt treatment of symptomatic infection.

Long-term antibiotic prophylaxis:

In children with recurrent UTIs or increased risk, low-dose long-term antibiotic prophylaxis can reduce the risk of febrile UTIs. Options include nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim, or an oral cephalosporin. Dosages: trimethoprim 2 mg/kg/day, nitrofurantoin 1–2 mg/kg/day, and cefaclor 10 mg/kg/day.

Circumcision:

Reduces the rate of UTIs in boys.

Treatment of bladder dysfunction:

Urotherapy, treatment of constipation, adequate fluid intake, regular voiding, biofeedback for dysfunctional voiding, and, when appropriate, medication with anticholinergic agents.

Follow-up:

Assess febrile UTIs, voiding symptoms, and constipation; perform a physical examination including growth assessment and blood pressure measurement, urinalysis, and ultrasound of the kidneys, urinary tract, and bladder with measurement of postvoid residual volume. Obtain a urine culture if symptoms are present or urinalysis suggests infection. VCUG (or an alternative) and renal scintigraphy are performed according to risk, based on reflux grade, UTIs, and renal function.

Evidence for conservative treatment and antibiotic prophylaxis:

Earlier randomized trials showed clinically relevant rates of spontaneous resolution with conservative treatment, particularly with lower-grade reflux, unilateral disease, and younger age. In children with grades I–IV VUR after a UTI, RIVUR found that trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole approximately halved the recurrence rate but did not significantly reduce new renal scars and was associated with more resistant breakthrough infections (Hoberman et al., 2014). PREDICT found that prophylaxis moderately reduced first UTI in infants with grades III–V VUR and no prior UTI, but showed no established benefit for renal scarring or function and was associated with more resistant infections (Morello et al., 2023). Continuous antibiotic prophylaxis should therefore be used according to individual risk.

Surgical Therapy

Indications:

Randomized trials support conservative treatment for many children with VUR. Consider surgery for frequent febrile breakthrough infections, persistent high-grade reflux, significant renal parenchymal damage, worsening renal function, poor adherence to or rejection of long-term antibiotic prophylaxis, or a preference for definitive treatment.

Endoscopic treatment:

Endoscopic injection of dextranomer/hyaluronic acid or another approved bulking agent beneath the refluxing orifice is the least invasive surgical option. The implant is placed in the submucosa. Several injection techniques are available. The STING technique injects beneath the ureteral orifice at the 6 o'clock position until the implant closes the orifice [fig. endoscopic treatment]. The HIT technique places the injection somewhat deeper in the ureter. Treatment success is assessed by cystography after injection; a repeat injection may be needed.

Endoscopic injection for a refluxing ureteral orifice (STING technique). The implant is injected into the submucosa beneath the orifice at the 6 o'clock position until the orifice appears closed (0.5–1.5 ml).

Retrospective comparisons with open surgery have reported similar results for endoscopic treatment (90 % resolution after two injections). Success rates are lower with higher-grade reflux, duplex systems, and neurogenic bladder. Because it is less invasive and open surgery remains an option if treatment fails, endoscopic treatment is an important option for patients without associated malformations.

Ureteroneocystostomy:

Ureteroneocystostomy can use an extravesical Lich-Gregoir technique or a transvesical Leadbetter-Politano or Cohen technique. Surgery can be performed by open surgery, laparoscopy, transvesicoscopy, or with robotic assistance. Laparoscopic procedures are generally performed using the extravesical Lich-Gregoir technique. Transvesicoscopic intravesical procedures using the Cohen or Leadbetter-Politano technique are possible but remain limited to specialized centers. Kidneys with relative split function above 10 % on renal scintigraphy are considered worth preserving. Long-term renal function in previously damaged kidneys usually remains stable or may improve slightly after ureteral reimplantation (see ureteral reimplantation).

Extravesical Lich-Gregoir technique:

The ureteral orifice is approached extravesically. The bladder muscle is incised without opening the mucosa; the ureter is placed in the muscular groove, and the muscle is closed over it. The risk of bladder denervation and voiding dysfunction after bilateral surgery remains controversial.

Transvesical Leadbetter-Politano technique:

The ureteral orifice is approached transvesically, circumferentially incised, and the ureter is dissected, with additional extravesical dissection if needed. After complete mobilization, a submucosal tunnel is created from the original orifice cranially. The ureter is passed through a new opening in the bladder wall approximately 3–4 cm cranial to the original orifice and through the tunnel, creating an antireflux implantation. The ureter again opens at the original orifice.

Transvesical Cohen technique:

The ureteral orifice is approached transvesically, circumferentially incised, and the ureter is mobilized. A submucosal tunnel is created transversely across the trigone; the ureter is drawn into the bladder and reimplanted. After bilateral reimplantation, the left orifice lies on the right and vice versa.

Other surgical options:

The following procedures may be considered, depending on renal function and associated malformations:

Heminephroureterectomy:

For a duplex ureter with a nonfunctioning refluxing renal segment.

Ureteroureterostomy:

Anastomosis of the distal end of the refluxing duplicated ureter to the competent ureter, with resection of the incompetent orifice.

Nephroureterectomy:

If relative renal function is below 10 %, consider laparoscopic nephrectomy with ureterectomy to prevent infections. Some authors question the need for ureteral resection.

Urinary diversion:

For absent bladder function, for example, in spina bifida; see urinary diversion.
