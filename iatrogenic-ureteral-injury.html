Diagnosis and Treatment of Iatrogenic Ureteral Injury

Definition:

Iatrogenic ureteral injuries result from intraoperative or interventional damage to the ureter.

Epidemiology:

Urologic procedures that may injure the ureter include ureteroscopy, radical prostatectomy or simple prostatectomy, transurethral resections, and pelvic lymphadenectomy. Common procedures in other specialties that may injure the ureter include hysterectomy, adnexal surgery, rectal surgery, sigmoid colon surgery, and vascular surgery (Elliott and McAninch, 2006; Preston, 2000).

Etiology:

Mechanisms include transection, incision into the ureter, perforation, ligation, thermal injury, contusion, and compression by a hematoma or lymphocele.

Signs and symptoms:

Clinical manifestations may include flank pain, urinary obstruction, urinoma, urinary ascites, fever, sepsis, elevated serum creatinine, anuria, or a ureterovaginal fistula.

Diagnosis:

Evaluation includes laboratory testing (complete blood count, serum creatinine, C-reactive protein, and urinalysis) and ultrasonography to detect urinary obstruction, a urinoma, or free fluid. When ureteral injury is suspected, contrast-enhanced CT urography with delayed-phase imaging is the most important cross-sectional imaging study. Cystoscopy and retrograde pyelography and/or antegrade pyelography establish the site of injury and may allow endoscopic treatment. A cystogram is also appropriate when bladder involvement is suspected.

Therapy

Ureteral stenting:

If possible, place an antegrade or retrograde double-J ureteral stent for ureteral strictures or a urinoma. The stent may be removed later, depending on healing. Persistent ureteral stricture requires surgical treatment. Percutaneous nephrostomy is an option when ureteral stent placement is not possible.

Surgical treatment:

Indications for surgical revision include inability to place a ureteral stent, urinary ascites, a ureterovaginal fistula, and persistent ureteral stricture after stenting. Reconstruction depends on the location and extent of the ureteral injury; see the section on persistent ureteral stricture. Depending on the clinical circumstances, a two-stage approach after percutaneous nephrostomy may be appropriate, for example in patients with peritonitis, sepsis, or shock.
