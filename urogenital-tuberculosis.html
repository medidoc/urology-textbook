Urogenital Tuberculosis: Symptoms, Diagnosis and Treatment

Definition

Urogenital tuberculosis is a usually chronic infection of the urogenital organs caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Review literature: (Figueiredo et al., 2025) (EAU Guidelines on Urological Infections).

Consumption

Consumption is a historical term for advanced tuberculosis.

Epidemiology

Tuberculosis is one of the most important infectious diseases worldwide. According to the WHO Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, an estimated 10.7 million people developed tuberculosis worldwide in 2024, and approximately 1.23 million died from the disease. The global prevalence of latent (asymptomatic) infection is approximately 25%.

Tuberculosis in Germany

In 2024, 4,391 new cases were reported, with an incidence of 5.2 per 100,000; the trend is declining. Mortality is 0.2 per 100,000; two-thirds of deaths occur in people older than 65 years. The male-to-female ratio is 2:1. A migration background (birth outside Germany) is a major risk factor for tuberculosis infection.

Urogenital tuberculosis accounts for 4% of new cases. The prevalence of renal tuberculosis in European autopsy studies is 0.6–0.9%. Tuberculosis is a notifiable disease by name in Germany.

Etiology

Pathogen

Mycobacterium tuberculosis is an acid-fast bacillus 1–2 μm long. It persists intracellularly in macrophages and replicates slowly.

Transmission

Tuberculosis is transmitted by respiratory droplets and primarily affects the lungs. During their lifetime, 5–10% of infected people develop active tuberculosis; the proportion is considerably higher in the presence of risk factors (see below). Fifty percent of disease episodes occur within the first two years.

In developing countries, primary infection of the intestinal tract through milk from infected cows remains relevant. Tuberculosis is very rarely transmitted through the skin, for example during autopsy or sexual intercourse.

Risk Factors

  • Origin outside the country
  • Alcoholism
  • Older age
  • AIDS
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Steroid therapy
  • Intravenous drug use
  • Smoking
  • Chronic lung diseases, including silicosis and COPD
  • Malnutrition
  • Malignant diseases
  • Gastric resection
  • Homelessness
  • Congregate living settings
  • Incarceration

Pathophysiology

Intracellular Persistence

M. tuberculosis is a typical example of a successful intracellular bacterium. The pathogen persists in macrophages. Several mechanisms protect the tubercle bacillus from enzymatic digestion in macrophages: suppression of oxygen radicals, suppression of phagosome acidification, and a waxy cell wall.

Primary Complex

The pathogen initially replicates in the lung tissue and lymph nodes. The immune system gradually gains control, primarily through its cellular component, and the pathogens become sequestered in granulomas. Depending on the immune status, the course may be subclinical (latent infection) or clinically apparent.

Disease Progression

Bacteremia may lead to additional foci (minimal lesions) in the lungs and other organs, which can serve as the source of later recurrences. With transient impairment of immune function, clinically apparent recurrence may arise from the primary complex or from minimal lesions.

Pathology

Minimal Lesions

Minimal lesions develop hematogenously as part of the primary complex or secondary involvement of the kidney or other urogenital organs. Caseating tuberculomas develop into calcified foci. The immune status determines the subsequent course.

Progressive Renal Infection

Increasing parenchymal and papillary destruction leads to papillary necrosis [fig. CT of urogenital tuberculosis], calyceal deformities, calyceal cavities [fig. urogenital tuberculosis on urography], infundibular stenoses, and scarring with renal atrophy.

Putty Kidney

The end stage of renal involvement is a putty kidney, which consists almost entirely of caseating necrosis and has no residual function.

Ureter

Strictures develop and rapidly lead to a nonfunctioning hydronephrotic kidney.

Urinary Bladder

Tuberculosis of the urinary bladder develops secondarily through pathogen shedding from the upper urinary tract. Initial findings include edematous and ulcerative changes of the bladder mucosa, followed by scarring, reduced compliance, and formation of a contracted bladder [fig. tuberculous contracted bladder]. Distal ureteral strictures and vesicoureteral reflux may also develop [fig. CT of urogenital tuberculosis].

Male Genital Tract

Initial tuberculomas in the epididymis develop hematogenously; renal involvement is not obligatory. The vas deferens, prostate, seminal vesicles, and, less commonly, the testis may become involved as the disease progresses. Fistula formation to the scrotal skin is possible.

Penis

The penis is rarely the site of primary infection after sexual contact with an infected partner who has genital or oral manifestations of tuberculosis. The initial lesion is a firm nodule or ulcer; biopsy or pathogen culture is required to distinguish it from a malignancy. Secondary tuberculosis of the penile skin is rare and corresponds to lupus vulgaris. It can result in destructive infection of the penis.

Histology

Different granulomas develop depending on the immune status:

  • Poor immune status (anergic): caseous necrosis without an immune response
  • Normal immune status: caseous necrosis surrounded by macrophages (epithelioid cells and Langhans giant cells)
  • Very good immune status (hyperergic): epithelioid cells and Langhans giant cells without central necrosis

Signs and Symptoms

Urogenital Tract

The main clinical manifestations are lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), flank pain, microscopic hematuria, arterial hypertension, and colic. Epididymal involvement causes swelling, pain, and redness. Infertility is also a concern. Penile tuberculosis is rare and presents as an ulcer (primary infection) or destructive inflammation (lupus vulgaris; see above).

Respiratory Tract

Symptoms include fever, cough, night sweats, erythema nodosum, pleurodynia, bloody sputum, and caseous pneumonia. In chronic disease, progressive fibrosis, amyloidosis, and cavitary carcinoma may cause respiratory insufficiency. Miliary tuberculosis is hematogenous dissemination in the setting of impaired immunity.

Musculoskeletal System

Manifestations include arthritis, vertebral involvement with gibbus formation, psoas abscess with spontaneous drainage through the groin, osteomyelitis, and spina ventosa (swelling due to involvement of the phalanges).

Other Organ Systems

Hematogenous dissemination can affect any organ.

Diagnosis

Tuberculin Skin Test

Intradermal injection of pathogen proteins causes a tissue reaction within 72 hours. Depending on the immune status and immune activation by an active infection, a papule of varying size with erythema develops. The tuberculin skin test demonstrates immunologic sensitization to mycobacteria but can neither confirm nor reliably exclude active urogenital tuberculosis. Its interpretation is limited after BCG vaccination.

In patients with competent immune function and no risk factors for infection, induration greater than 15 mm is suspicious for infection. In patients with immunodeficiency or risk factors for infection, smaller indurations (5–10 mm) are suspicious.

Interferon-Gamma Release Assay

Antigens from Mycobacterium tuberculosis stimulate the cellular immune system in a blood sample in vitro. If the patient has previously been exposed to tuberculosis bacteria, increased interferon-γ is released into the sample and detected by ELISA. The interferon-gamma release assay is particularly useful in patients with a history of BCG vaccination.

Urine Sediment and Urine Culture

Leukocyturia is present. Mycobacteria may sometimes be detected in the sediment after special staining. Urine culture is typically sterile; however, detection of other bacteria does not rule out tuberculosis.

Urine Culture for Tuberculosis

Morning urine is sent in special culture media on three consecutive days. Culture with susceptibility testing takes at least four weeks. A positive urine culture has high specificity. A negative urine culture does not exclude the disease because of limited sensitivity.

PCR Testing of Urine

PCR or nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) enables rapid detection. Rifampicin resistance can also be determined (Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra). Positive results, which have high specificity, are available within hours; a negative test does not exclude tuberculosis because of limited sensitivity.

Pulmonary Evaluation

Concomitant pulmonary tuberculosis should be actively investigated even in the absence of respiratory symptoms, using history, chest radiography, and sputum microscopy, NAAT, and culture.

Ultrasound

Ultrasound may show renal calcifications, caliectasis, hydronephrosis, renal abscesses, and atrophic kidneys. Epididymal involvement appears as mixed-echogenicity enlargement of the epididymis; testicular involvement is usually a hypoechoic mass. A normal ultrasound does not exclude early urogenital tuberculosis.

Computed Tomography

CT is the most important imaging modality in confirmed or suspected urogenital tuberculosis. Typical findings include hypoperfused areas of renal parenchyma, calcifications in the urogenital organs, calyceal deformities, calyceal cavities, hydronephrosis, parenchymal scars, ureteral strictures, and bladder wall thickening.

Urography

Conventional excretory urography has become much less important than CT urography but may show characteristic calyceal deformities, calyceal cavities, infundibular stenoses, nonvisualization of calyces, or ureteral strictures [fig. urogenital tuberculosis on urography].

Retrograde Pyelography

Retrograde pyelography is used to evaluate a ureteral stricture, in patients with renal insufficiency, and before planned internal ureteral stenting [fig. tuberculous contracted bladder].

Renal Scintigraphy

Renal scintigraphy is used to assess renal function in advanced disease.

Cystoscopy and Ureteroscopy

Cystoscopy and ureteroscopy are indicated for unclear findings, hematuria, suspected tumor, or before planned reconstruction; biopsy may be performed as needed.

Notifiable Disease

Detection of mycobacteria is a notifiable event by name in Germany.

Treatment

Standard Treatment

Initial standard therapy for tuberculosis consists of isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for two months. Subsequent treatment is determined by culture and susceptibility results; for example, isoniazid and rifampin may be given for at least four months, with a different combination if indicated by the results. Treatment for uncomplicated pulmonary or urogenital tuberculosis lasts six months. Before treatment is discontinued, clear clinical evidence of success should be documented (negative urine cultures and improvement on imaging). See also Pharmacology, Adverse Effects, and Dosing of Antituberculosis Drugs.

Monitoring During Antituberculosis Therapy

Before treatment and, if well tolerated, at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 weeks, obtain a complete laboratory panel including a complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, and uric acid. Ethambutol therapy requires ophthalmologic monitoring. Streptomycin carries a risk of irreversible hearing impairment and tinnitus; otolaryngologic monitoring is necessary. Pyridoxine (vitamin B complex) is prescribed to prevent neurologic adverse effects.

Drug Resistance

Rifampicin resistance can be diagnosed rapidly by NAAT. Otherwise, treatment is modified when susceptibility results become available. Treatment duration depends on the response and the availability of newer treatment options (BPaLM regimen: bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, and moxifloxacin for six months). Management of drug-resistant tuberculosis should be coordinated with an experienced tuberculosis center.

Isolation

Cooperative patients with exclusively urogenital involvement do not need to be isolated; however, a separate bathroom and toilet are recommended until the pathogen is no longer detectable in the urine. A gown, mask, and gloves are required when handling pathogen-containing secretions. Patients with infectious pulmonary tuberculosis should be isolated in a single room; staff and visitors should wear a gown, mask, and gloves until the pathogen is no longer detectable in sputum.

Follow-Up

After treatment is completed, urine cultures for tuberculosis are obtained every three months for one year.

Renal Abscess

Percutaneous drainage or nephrectomy, depending on the extent of disease and renal function.

Obstructed Kidney

Early urinary drainage with a double-J ureteral stent or percutaneous nephrostomy is important to preserve renal function.

Surgery

At least six weeks of antituberculosis pretreatment should be considered before surgical intervention.

Nephrectomy

Nephrectomy is indicated for a nonfunctioning kidney.

Ureteral Strictures

Begin with six months of medical therapy and ureteral stenting. If hydronephrosis persists despite good renal function, surgical repair is indicated according to the location and extent of the stricture: endoscopic ureterotomy, Anderson-Hynes pyeloplasty, end-to-end anastomosis, ureteral reimplantation, or ileal ureter replacement.

Epididymectomy

Epididymectomy is indicated for treatment-refractory epididymitis.

Bladder Augmentation

Bladder augmentation is indicated for a scarred, contracted bladder with very low capacity and significant symptoms. Cystectomy may be considered for an extremely contracted bladder with complex involvement of the trigone and distal ureters.
